Live Cricket Scores in Pakistan: How to Follow Every Ball (2026 Guide)

By Anwaar Digital · Last updated: 20 November 2026 · Cricket & live scores

Cricket is Pakistan's favourite conversation. When the national team plays, offices slow down, shops turn up the television, and millions of people refresh their phones after every ball. But following a match live is not always simple — the television may be busy, the internet may be slow, or you may be travelling and only have a weak mobile signal.

This guide explains how to follow live cricket scores in Pakistan in plain English: the best places to check ball-by-ball scores, how to read a scorecard even if you are new to the game, which apps and TV channels carry Pakistan matches, and how to keep up on a slow connection without using up all your mobile data. No sign-up needed, no fake numbers — just the places and habits that actually work.

How live scores actually work

A live score is not one number — it is a small stream of updates. Every ball bowled produces new information: the runs scored, whether a wicket fell, who is batting, and how many overs are left. Score websites and apps receive this data from official scorers at the ground and push it to your screen, usually within a few seconds of the ball being bowled.

That small delay matters. A score app is almost never exactly in step with the television picture — it is usually a few seconds ahead or behind. So if a friend shouts from the next room, do not be surprised if your phone has not shown the wicket yet, or has shown it a moment early. The delay is normal, not a fault in the app.

There are three kinds of live coverage, and they suit different situations:

  • Ball-by-ball text commentary. A written line for every ball — "good length, defended", "FOUR, driven through cover" — plus the updated score. This is the lightest option and works on the slowest connections.
  • Live scorecard. The full score: both teams, every batter's runs and balls, every bowler's figures, fall of wickets and the run rate. Best when you want the whole picture at a glance.
  • Live video or audio. Full streaming or radio commentary. Best experience, but uses the most data and needs a strong connection.

Most fans use a mix: text commentary when they are out and about, and video when they are home on Wi-Fi. The sections below show where to find each one.

Where to follow live scores

Start with the official sources. The Pakistan Cricket Board (PCB) publishes live scores, fixtures and squads for Pakistan's home matches and domestic cricket on its own website and app, and the International Cricket Council (ICC) covers international tournaments — World Cups, the Champions Trophy and the World Test Championship — with official live scores and match centres. Official sources are the most reliable because the data comes straight from the match scorers.

Then there are the big score apps and websites, which cover every match in the world, including domestic cricket in Pakistan, the Pakistan Super League (PSL), county cricket and franchise leagues. The best-known names are ESPNcricinfo and Cricbuzz, and most fans in Pakistan keep one of these two on their phone. Both offer ball-by-ball commentary, full scorecards, live win-probability estimates and match alerts. Cricbuzz is popular for its fast, light app; ESPNcricinfo is popular for its deep coverage, statistics and expert analysis.

Sports news apps also carry live scores — the sports sections of major news apps and Google's own sports score cards show the live score when you search for a match. These are handy when you do not want to install a cricket app, but they are usually slower and less detailed than a dedicated score app.

How to read a scorecard (in plain words)

A scorecard looks complicated the first time you see it, but it is really just a short list of answers to simple questions. Here are the terms you will meet most often:

  • PAK 187/4 (32.3 overs). Pakistan have scored 187 runs, lost 4 wickets, and faced 32 overs and 3 balls. The number before the slash is runs; after it, wickets. An over is 6 balls, so "32.3" means 32 full overs plus 3 balls.
  • Run rate. Runs per over. A run rate of 5.80 means the team is scoring just under 6 runs per over. In a chase, you will also see the required run rate — the rate needed from here to win.
  • Batter: 64 (52). The batter has scored 64 runs from 52 balls. The number in brackets is balls faced. A dot ball is a ball with no run; a "4" or "6" is a boundary.
  • Bowler: 2/31 (7 overs). The bowler has taken 2 wickets and given away 31 runs in 7 overs. The first number is wickets, the second is runs.
  • Strike rate. For a batter, runs per 100 balls — a strike rate of 130 means fast scoring. For a bowler, balls per wicket — a lower number is better.
  • Fall of wickets: 45-1, 112-2. The score when each wicket fell. It tells you the story of the innings at a glance — a steady climb, or a collapse.
  • DLS / Duckworth-Lewis-Stern. In rain-affected limited-overs matches, the target is recalculated. If you see "DLS par score", that is the score the chasing team needs to be ahead of when play stops.
  • Net run rate (NRR). Used in tournaments to separate teams level on points. It is the team's scoring rate minus the rate they concede. You do not need to calculate it — just know that a big win improves it and a heavy defeat hurts it.
  • Powerplay. In limited-overs cricket, the first few overs have fielding restrictions that favour the batters — 10 overs in a one-day match, 6 in a T20. Scores usually come faster during the powerplay.

Once you know these nine terms, you can read any scorecard in the world. The rest is detail you can pick up match by match.

Following Pakistan matches specifically

Pakistan matches get the biggest crowds — and the most coverage. For a Pakistan home series, the PCB's channels are the first place to check for the squad, the schedule and the toss. For away series and ICC tournaments, the ICC match centre and the two big score apps cover every ball, usually with Urdu-language commentary options on some platforms.

On television in Pakistan, Pakistan matches are usually carried by the sports channels that hold the broadcast rights for that series — the rights change from series to series, so check the listing for the specific tour rather than assuming one channel always has it. If you are abroad, the rights sit with different broadcasters in each country, and the PCB or ICC match page usually lists who is showing the match where you are.

Radio still matters. When the television is not available — on a long drive, at work, or during a power cut — live radio commentary and audio streams on score apps keep you in the match on very little data. Before the match, check which station or app carries audio for that series, so you are not searching while the first over is already being bowled.

Match timings are often given in Pakistan Standard Time (PKT) on Pakistani sources and in local time on international ones. If a match is in Australia or England, the start time in Pakistan can be early morning or late evening — check the fixture time in PKT before you set an alarm. And remember that weather can delay a start: if it is raining at the ground, the scorecard will show the delay and the revised start time, and the GlobalStream weather page can tell you the conditions in the host city.

Low-data options: following a match on a slow connection

Many fans in Pakistan follow matches on mobile data, and video streaming can eat a whole data bundle in a single innings. If your connection is slow or your data is limited, use these habits:

  • Use text commentary, not video. Ball-by-ball text uses almost no data — a whole day's play is less than a few minutes of video. This is the single biggest saving you can make.
  • Turn off auto-play and images. Most score apps have a "data saver" or "lite" mode. Turn it on before the match, not during it.
  • Use score alerts, not constant refreshing. Set the app to notify you at wickets, milestones (a fifty or a hundred) and at the end of each innings. You get the big moments without keeping the app open all day.
  • Refresh manually on 2G or weak 3G. Auto-refresh every few seconds can fail and retry on a weak signal, wasting data. A manual refresh every over is enough.
  • Download the fixture list on Wi-Fi. Save the schedule before the series starts, so you are not loading pages just to check when the next match begins.
  • Listen to radio commentary. Audio uses far less data than video and keeps you in the match while you do other things.

Match schedules: never miss a match

Pakistan play three formats, and each has its own rhythm:

  • Test matches last up to five days, usually starting in the morning and running through the day with lunch and tea breaks. The score changes slowly — check at the breaks and at stumps.
  • One Day Internationals (ODIs) last a full day, about 50 overs per side. The match usually finishes in the evening. The middle overs are the quiet phase; the last ten overs of each innings are where the big scoring happens.
  • T20 matches last about three to four hours, 20 overs per side. Everything happens fast — a match can turn in a single over. This is the format where live alerts matter most, because missing ten minutes can mean missing the whole match.

The best habit is to check the fixture list the day before a series starts. Note the format, the start time in PKT, and the venue. Then set one reminder for the toss — 30 minutes before the first ball — because the toss decides who bats first and shapes the whole match. The PCB and ICC fixture pages, and both big score apps, publish the full schedule weeks ahead, and they update it if a match is rescheduled for weather or another reason.

Domestic cricket and the PSL have their own schedules. The PSL usually runs for about a month, with matches almost every evening — easy to follow if you check the day's fixture each morning. Domestic first-class and one-day matches get less television coverage, but the score apps carry them ball by ball, and the PCB publishes their scores too.

Fake streams and score scams — stay safe

Big matches attract scammers. The most common traps are fake "free live streaming" links shared on social media and messaging groups. They promise the match for free, then ask you to download an app, enter your phone number, or pay a small "activation" fee. The stream never appears — but your data, or your money, is gone.

  • Never download a streaming app from a link in a message. Use only the official app store, and only apps from broadcasters you recognise.
  • Never pay to "unlock" a free stream. Real broadcasters do not ask for payment through a chat message or a random link.
  • Never enter your bank or card details on a streaming site. If a "live score" page asks for payment details, close it — a scorecard never needs them.
  • Check the web address. Fake sites copy the look of real score sites with a slightly different address. If the address looks wrong, it is wrong.
  • Be careful with "win a prize" cricket messages. Messages claiming you have won tickets or a signed bat are almost always scams designed to steal your details.

Frequently asked questions

Where can I check live cricket scores for free?

The PCB and ICC websites publish official live scores for free, and the big score apps — ESPNcricinfo and Cricbuzz — are free with ads. No payment is needed to see a live scorecard.

Why is my score app behind the television?

Every source has a small delay of a few seconds. Television, radio and apps all receive the data through different routes, so they rarely match exactly. A delay of a few seconds is normal.

What is the difference between a live score and ball-by-ball commentary?

The live score is just the numbers — runs, wickets, overs. Ball-by-ball commentary adds a written description of every ball, so you can follow the match without watching it.

Can I follow a match with no internet at all?

Yes — live radio commentary works without the internet, and television needs only a signal, not data. If you have a basic phone, SMS score services from some networks can also send the score by text, though these depend on your mobile operator.

How do I know the start time of a Pakistan match in my city?

Check the fixture on the PCB or ICC page, which shows the time in Pakistan Standard Time, or on a score app, which usually converts it to your phone's time zone automatically. For prayer timings around match time, the GlobalStream prayer times page shows the day's timings for your city.

The bottom line

Following live cricket in Pakistan is easy once you have the right habits: keep one reliable score app on your phone, know how to read a scorecard, use text commentary when data is short, and check the fixture list the day before. Start with the official PCB and ICC pages for the facts, use a big score app for ball-by-ball detail, and watch out for fake streaming links — a real score never asks for your card details.
