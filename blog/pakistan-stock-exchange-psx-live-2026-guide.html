Pakistan Stock Exchange (PSX) Live: How to Read Market Rates and What Moves Them (2026 Guide)

By Anwaar Digital · Last updated: 21 November 2026 · Money & markets

Every weekday, thousands of Pakistanis check the same number before anything else: the KSE-100 index. It is the first headline on business news, the first figure on the Pakistan Stock Exchange website, and the number friends quote when they say "the market went up today" or "the market crashed". Yet most people who check it every day cannot explain what it actually measures — what an index point is, what trading volume means, or why the market jumps on a day when nothing seems to have happened.

This guide explains the Pakistan Stock Exchange (PSX) in plain English: what it is, how to read live PSX rates, what the numbers mean, what moves the market up and down, and how a beginner can follow it safely. You will not find a printed index value here — the market moves every second, so this guide shows you where to check the live figure instead. That is the whole point: learn to read the market yourself, rather than trusting a number someone copied yesterday.

What is the Pakistan Stock Exchange?

The Pakistan Stock Exchange, or PSX, is the country's national stock market. It was formed in 2016, when the Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad stock exchanges merged into one. Its main trading floor and headquarters are in Karachi, and it is where shares of Pakistani companies are bought and sold.

A share is a small piece of ownership in a company. When a company needs money to grow, it sells shares to the public — this is called listing on the stock exchange. Investors then buy and sell those shares among themselves. The price of a share goes up when more people want to buy it than sell it, and down when the opposite is true. The PSX lists hundreds of companies, from big banks and oil companies to cement makers, fertiliser plants, textile mills and power producers.

The PSX is regulated by the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP). Brokers who trade on the exchange must be licensed, and listed companies must publish their financial results regularly. That regulation does not make investing safe — prices still fall — but it does mean the market runs on published rules, and that anyone selling you shares without a licence is breaking the law.

What is the KSE-100 index?

The KSE-100 is the PSX's benchmark index, and the number most Pakistanis mean when they talk about "the market". It tracks the 100 largest and most actively traded companies on the exchange, and combines their share prices into a single number, measured in points.

When the news says "the KSE-100 gained 500 points today", it means the combined value of those 100 companies rose during the trading session. When it "lost 800 points", the combined value fell. A single point has no fixed rupee value — the index is a yardstick, not a price tag. What matters is the direction and the size of the move compared with recent days: a 1,000-point move on a quiet day means far more than the same move on a day when the whole market is swinging.

The KSE-100 is dominated by a few big sectors. Banks, oil and gas companies, cement makers, fertiliser producers and power companies carry the most weight, so their results move the index more than a small textile company does. That is why the index can rise even while many small shares fall — the heavyweights are pulling it up. There are other indices too: the KSE-30 tracks 30 leading companies, and the KMI-30 tracks Shariah-compliant companies for investors who want to avoid interest-based businesses.

How to read live PSX rates

Checking the live market takes less than a minute, once you know where to look:

  • Start with the official source. The Pakistan Stock Exchange website at psx.com.pk shows the live KSE-100 index, the day's top gainers and losers, trading volume and market announcements. It is the one source every other website is ultimately quoting.
  • Check the time stamp. Always look for the "last updated" time next to the number. Free market data is often delayed by several minutes, and a number without a time stamp is useless during trading hours.
  • Read the market watch table. The table lists each company's share price, how much it moved today (in rupees and in percent), and how many shares changed hands. The percent move matters more than the rupee move — a Rs 5 rise on a Rs 50 share is a big day; on a Rs 500 share, it is not.
  • Look at the gainers and losers. The top gainers list shows which shares rose most; the top losers shows which fell most. If one sector fills the gainers list — say, banks — you know where the day's buying is concentrated.
  • Follow the news behind the move. A market move without a reason is a mystery; a market move with a reason is information. The Pakistan news page and the world news page on GlobalStream show the headlines that usually explain the day's trading — a budget announcement, a policy-rate decision, an IMF statement, or a big company's results.

Trading takes place on weekdays, Monday to Friday, during the market hours published on the PSX website. On weekends and public holidays the market is closed, and the number you see is Friday's closing value. If someone quotes you a "live" PSX rate on a Sunday, they are quoting an old number.

What the numbers mean: points, volume and market capitalisation

Four numbers tell you most of what you need to know about a trading day:

1. Index points

The level of the KSE-100 itself. Watch the change in points and in percent from the previous close, not just the headline number. A new all-time high sounds dramatic, but it is normal for a growing market — the index is supposed to rise over the years as companies grow.

2. Volume — how many shares traded

Volume is the number of shares that changed hands during the day. High volume on a rising day means many investors were buying with confidence. High volume on a falling day means many were selling in a hurry. Low volume means few people were trading at all, and the day's move may not mean much.

3. Market capitalisation — the total value of the market

Market capitalisation, or market cap, is the total value of all listed companies put together: each company's share price multiplied by its number of shares, added up across the whole exchange. When the market cap rises, Pakistan's listed companies are worth more in total; when it falls, they are worth less. Journalists often quote it when they say the market "lost billions" in a day — that is the change in total market cap, not cash that anyone lost directly.

4. Value traded — how much money changed hands

This is the rupee value of all the shares bought and sold in a day. Together with volume, it tells you whether the market was busy or quiet. A busy day with big value traded usually follows big news.

Two more figures help you judge a single company. The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) compares a share's price with the company's yearly profit per share — a low P/E can mean a bargain or a company in trouble, and a high P/E can mean high hopes or a bubble. The dividend yield is the yearly dividend a company pays, shown as a percent of the share price — it is the income you get simply for holding the share. Both are shown on the PSX website and in broker apps, and both are worth checking before you judge any share as "cheap" or "expensive".

What moves the market?

The PSX does not move randomly. Six forces do most of the pushing:

1. Interest rates

This is the biggest single driver. When the State Bank of Pakistan raises its policy rate, bank deposits pay more, so saving becomes attractive and shares become less attractive — the market usually falls. When rates are cut, money flows out of bank deposits and into shares, and the market usually rises. That is why the whole market watches every monetary-policy announcement.

2. The rupee

Many listed companies earn or spend in dollars. Exporters such as textile mills earn more in rupees when the rupee weakens; importers such as oil companies pay more. So a sharp move in the dollar-to-rupee exchange rate pushes different sectors in different directions. Check the live currency rates on GlobalStream next to the market — the two usually move together.

3. Oil prices

Pakistan imports most of its oil, so world oil prices feed straight into inflation, the import bill and the rupee. High oil prices hurt the economy but can help oil exploration companies listed on the PSX. You can follow the cost that reaches ordinary people on the petrol price page.

4. Politics and policy

Budgets, tax changes, IMF programme news and political uncertainty move the market fast, because they change what investors expect companies to earn. A budget that raises taxes on banks hits bank shares the same day; news of an IMF agreement usually lifts the whole market, because it signals that the country's finances are stabilising.

5. Company results

Every few months, listed companies publish their profits. A company that earns far more than expected usually sees its share price jump; one that misses expectations usually falls. Results season is when careful investors do their homework — reading results is more useful than following daily price noise.

6. Global markets and foreign investors

When world markets fall — for example, after a big world news event — foreign investors often sell shares in smaller markets like Pakistan first, and the PSX falls with them. When global sentiment is strong, foreign money flows back in. Like gold, shares are a risk asset: in fearful times, money moves towards safer places such as gold, and in confident times it moves back into shares. Crypto is an even more volatile alternative — crypto prices can swing in a day by more than the PSX moves in a month, which is why most families treat shares as the middle ground between gold and crypto.

How a beginner follows the market safely

You do not need to buy a single share to learn the market. In fact, watching first is the smartest way to start:

  • Watch for a month before you spend anything. Follow the KSE-100 every day, read the business headlines, and try to explain each day's move in one sentence. If you cannot explain why the market moved, you are not ready to trade on it.
  • Make a watchlist. Pick five well-known companies — a bank, a cement maker, an oil company, a fertiliser company and a food company — and follow their prices and results for a few weeks. You will learn more from five companies you understand than from a hundred you do not.
  • Practise without money. Write down the shares you would buy and the price you would pay, then check a month later whether you would have gained or lost. This "paper trading" teaches the same lessons as real trading, without the losses.
  • Use a licensed broker and a CDC account. To buy shares in Pakistan you need an account with a licensed broker and an investor account with the Central Depository Company (CDC), which holds your shares electronically. Open accounts only with SECP-licensed brokers — check the licence on the SECP website before you deposit any money.
  • Start small and spread the risk. Never put all your money in one share, and never borrow money to buy shares. Most experienced investors hold several different companies, so that one bad result cannot wipe them out.
  • Think in years, not days. The PSX rewards patient investors who hold good companies and let profits grow. Day trading — buying and selling within hours — looks exciting, but most beginners who try it lose money to fees and quick decisions.
  • Do the maths first. Before you buy, work out what the numbers mean — profit, dividend yield, how much you are actually paying. The free calculators on Anwaar Free Tools can help with the arithmetic, but the decision is yours.

Risks and scams to avoid

  • Prices can fall — and stay down. Shares are not bank deposits. A share can lose half its value and take years to recover. Never invest money you will need soon for rent, fees or a wedding.
  • Ignore "guaranteed profit" tips. Nobody can guarantee a return on shares. WhatsApp and Telegram groups that promise fixed monthly profits, or sell "insider tips" for a fee, are almost always scams — the people running them profit from your subscription, not from the tips. Real market information is public and free.
  • Beware of pump-and-dump schemes. A group buys a cheap, little-known share quietly, then hypes it online so others rush in and push the price up — and then the group sells and the price collapses. If a stranger urges you to buy an unknown share "before it is too late", that is the warning sign, not the opportunity.
  • Never share your account login. No broker, no CDC officer and no "market expert" will ever ask for your password or OTP. Anyone who does is trying to steal your account. If you want to learn how to spot fake websites and fake investment pages, the free guides on CyberSec Hub show you what to check before you trust or pay anyone online.
  • Check the licence. Before you give money to any broker, adviser or investment app, confirm it is licensed by the SECP. Unlicensed operators are not just risky — they are illegal, and if they disappear with your money, there is usually no way to get it back.

Frequently asked questions

When does the PSX open?

Trading takes place on weekdays, Monday to Friday, during the market hours published on the PSX website at psx.com.pk. The market is closed on weekends and public holidays.

What is a "good" KSE-100 level?

There is no fixed good or bad level. The index is supposed to rise over the years as companies grow, so today's level only means something compared with last month, last year, or the level when you started watching. A better question is whether company profits are growing — if they are, the index usually follows.

Can I buy shares with a small amount of money?

Yes. Many listed shares trade for only a few rupees each, so you can start with a small amount once your broker and CDC accounts are open. But remember that broker fees apply to every trade, so very small trades lose a bigger share to fees — another reason to start by watching and learning.

Is trading on the PSX halal?

That depends on which companies you buy and on your own religious judgement. The KMI-30 index lists Shariah-compliant companies that avoid interest-based business, alcohol and other prohibited activities. Scholars differ on the details of share trading, so if this matters to you, ask a qualified scholar you trust rather than taking a stranger's word online.

Where can I check the live PSX rate for free?

On the PSX website itself, psx.com.pk — the official source, with the index, market watch, gainers and losers, and company announcements. For the news behind the numbers, check the Pakistan news and world news pages on GlobalStream.

The bottom line

The Pakistan Stock Exchange is not a casino and not a mystery. It is a market where the ownership of real Pakistani companies changes hands, and its numbers — index points, volume, market capitalisation — simply describe what thousands of buyers and sellers did today. The smart way to follow it is to check the live rate from the official source, understand which force is moving it — interest rates, the rupee, oil, politics, results or world markets — and to watch and learn before you spend a rupee.

Check the live dollar rate and the gold rate alongside the market, keep an eye on Pakistan news, and remember that no guide, no tip and no video can replace your own homework.
