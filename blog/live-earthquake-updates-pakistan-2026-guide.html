Live Earthquake Updates in Pakistan: How to Check and Stay Safe (2026 Guide)

By Anwaar Digital · Last updated: 25 November 2026 · Safety & live information

An earthquake does not send a warning. One moment everything is normal, the next the floor is moving, the walls are shaking, and everyone in the room is asking the same question: how strong was it, and where was it? In Pakistan, that question matters more than in most countries. Large parts of the country sit in an active earthquake zone, and tremors are felt somewhere in Pakistan almost every year — sometimes lightly, sometimes with serious damage.

This guide explains live earthquake updates in Pakistan in plain English: where to check a tremor the moment you feel one, how to read the numbers in an earthquake report, what to do during and after shaking, and how to prepare your home and family before the next one. No printed magnitudes or dates here — earthquakes change by the minute, so this guide shows you where to check them live, from official sources, and how to tell real information from rumours.

Why does Pakistan shake so often?

Pakistan sits where two of the Earth's great tectonic plates meet: the Indian plate pushing northwards into the Eurasian plate. The collision that built the Himalayas and the Hindu Kush is still happening, very slowly, and the pressure it creates is released as earthquakes. That is why the northern areas — Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Gilgit-Baltistan, Azad Kashmir and the areas near the Hindu Kush — feel tremors more often than the plains of Punjab or the deserts of Sindh, and why Balochistan, which sits on its own fault systems, has also seen major earthquakes in its history.

Most tremors in Pakistan are small and cause no damage. They are felt for a few seconds and pass. But the same geology means a strong earthquake is always possible, and history shows that when a big one strikes, it can affect whole cities. That is the reason to know, before anything happens, where to check reliable information and what to do — the same habit as checking the weather forecast before a storm or live flights before a journey. Preparation costs nothing; panic costs lives.

Where to check live earthquake updates in Pakistan

When you feel a tremor, do not wait for a WhatsApp forward. Check an official source. These are the most reliable places to look:

1. Pakistan Meteorological Department (PMD)

The PMD is Pakistan's official earthquake authority. Its National Seismic Monitoring Centre records tremors across the country and publishes the location, magnitude and depth of each event. Search for "PMD earthquake" or "Pakistan Meteorological Department seismic" and look for the official pmd.gov.pk website. The PMD report is the one Pakistani news channels quote first, and it is the best source for the exact epicentre inside Pakistan.

2. USGS Earthquake Map

The United States Geological Survey (USGS) runs the world's most used earthquake map at earthquake.usgs.gov. It lists earthquakes worldwide within minutes of them happening, with magnitude, depth, location and a "Did You Feel It?" map showing where people reported shaking. It is the fastest way to confirm a tremor you felt and to see whether it was centred in Pakistan or in a neighbouring country, such as Afghanistan or the Hindu Kush region, where many tremors felt in northern Pakistan actually begin.

3. EMSC (European-Mediterranean Seismological Centre)

The EMSC website and its LastQuake app are popular in Pakistan because they are fast and easy to read. They show the latest earthquakes on a map, let you filter by region, and collect felt reports from ordinary people — so within minutes of a tremor you can see how many others felt it and where. The app also sends alerts for earthquakes near your location.

4. Android earthquake alerts

Most Android phones in Pakistan have a built-in earthquake alert system, switched on by default in the phone's safety settings. It uses the phone's sensors and Google's detection network to warn you a few seconds before shaking arrives from a distant epicentre — seconds that are enough to move away from a window or under a table. Check that it is enabled: Settings, then Safety & emergency, then Earthquake alerts. iPhone users can get similar alerts through the EMSC LastQuake app or the USGS feed.

5. NDMA and official news

The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) issues guidance after significant earthquakes, including safety advice and helpline information. For the wider picture — damage reports, rescue efforts and official statements — the Pakistan news page and the world news page on GlobalStream collect the latest verified reporting in one place.

One warning: social media is the fastest source and the least reliable one. A tremor in one city is often reported within minutes as a "major earthquake" in a city hundreds of kilometres away, with invented magnitudes and fake casualty figures. Always confirm what you read on social media against PMD, USGS or EMSC before sharing it — and never forward a message that predicts a future earthquake. No scientist, app or astrologer can predict the date, time and place of an earthquake. Any message claiming to do so is false.

How to read an earthquake report

An earthquake report looks technical, but it has only four numbers that matter:

Magnitude

Magnitude measures the energy released at the source, on a scale where each whole number is roughly 32 times more energy than the one below it. Below 4 is usually felt lightly or not at all. Between 4 and 5 is clearly felt and can rattle windows. Between 5 and 6 can damage weak buildings. Above 6 can cause serious damage, and above 7 is a major earthquake. A magnitude 5.5 and a magnitude 6.5 are not "one point apart" in effect — the second releases about 32 times more energy than the first.

Depth

Depth is how far below the surface the earthquake started. Shallow earthquakes (less than 70 km deep) are usually felt more strongly and cause more damage at the surface. Deep earthquakes, which are common in the Hindu Kush region, can be felt over a very wide area — people in Islamabad, Lahore and Peshawar may all feel the same deep tremor — but they usually cause less damage than a shallow one of the same magnitude.

Epicentre

The epicentre is the point on the Earth's surface directly above where the earthquake started. Reports name the nearest town or district, and the distance from it — for example, "20 km north of Chitral". The closer you are to the epicentre, the stronger the shaking. If the epicentre is in Afghanistan's Hindu Kush, a tremor felt in Peshawar may barely be noticed in Karachi.

Time — and the time zone trap

International sources like USGS report earthquake times in UTC (Coordinated Universal Time). Pakistan Standard Time (PKT) is UTC + 5 hours. So a USGS time of 14:00 UTC means 19:00 PKT — 7 in the evening in Pakistan. If you are comparing a report with the time you felt the shaking, add 5 hours to the UTC time, or check whether the source already shows local time. The PMD reports in Pakistan time directly. What to do during an earthquake

Most earthquake injuries do not come from buildings collapsing. They come from falling objects, broken glass and people running in panic. The international guidance is simple and it saves lives:

  • Drop, Cover and Hold On. Drop to your hands and knees, cover your head and neck under a sturdy table or desk, and hold on until the shaking stops. If there is no table, crouch against an inside wall, away from windows, and cover your head with your arms.
  • Stay indoors if you are indoors. Do not run outside during the shaking. Stairwells, exits and building entrances are where debris falls. Most injuries happen to people moving during the tremor, not to people who stay put.
  • Stay away from windows, mirrors and heavy furniture. Glass shatters, shelves topple and wall hangings fall. Move away from them if you can do so in one or two steps; otherwise, protect your head.
  • If you are outside, move to open ground. Get away from buildings, walls, trees, poles and overhead wires, then drop and cover your head.
  • If you are driving, stop safely. Pull over away from bridges, flyovers, poles and buildings, stay in the car with your seatbelt on, and wait until the shaking stops.
  • If you are in a lift, do not use it. If the shaking starts while you are in a lift, press every floor button and get out at the first floor where the doors open. Never use a lift after an earthquake until it has been checked.
  • Do not light a match or a lighter. A gas line may have broken. Use a torch, not a flame, if you need light.

Shaking usually lasts only seconds — rarely more than a minute. Count slowly and wait for it to stop completely before moving. Children should be taught the same three words at home and at school: drop, cover, hold on. A drill at home, practised once, is worth more than a hundred warnings read on a phone.

After the tremor: what to check first

  • Expect aftershocks. Smaller tremors often follow a larger one, sometimes for hours or days. Each time, repeat drop, cover and hold on. Aftershocks can bring down buildings already weakened by the first shock.
  • Check yourself and others for injuries. Help the injured first, and call emergency services if anyone is seriously hurt. Do not move a badly injured person unless they are in danger where they lie.
  • Check for gas, electricity and water damage. Smell for gas. If you smell it, open windows, leave the building, and do not switch anything electrical on or off. If you see sparks, broken wires or a damaged meter, switch off the main power if it is safe to reach. Do not drink tap water until the supply is confirmed safe — a broken pipe can contaminate it.
  • Do not re-enter a damaged building. Cracks in walls, leaning walls, a shifted foundation or a sagging roof are warning signs. Stay out and wait for an official inspection. In a city, damaged buildings can also shed debris onto the street, so keep your distance.
  • Use your phone wisely. Send one short message to family saying you are safe, then keep calls brief — networks jam after an earthquake, and emergency services need the lines. Text messages get through more easily than voice calls. Keep your phone charged; if the power is out, save battery for essential use.
  • Follow official information only. Check the PMD, USGS, EMSC and NDMA, and trusted news. Ignore forwarded messages predicting another earthquake — aftershocks are expected, but their exact time and size cannot be predicted, and rumours cause more harm than the shaking itself.
  • If you are near the coast and feel strong shaking, move to high ground. Pakistan's Makran coast has a tsunami risk after a large undersea earthquake. If the sea suddenly pulls back, or you receive an official tsunami warning, move inland and uphill immediately and do not wait to see the wave.

If your home is safe and your family is together, check on elderly neighbours and anyone living alone nearby. In every major earthquake, neighbours are the first rescuers — long before official teams arrive.

Your earthquake emergency kit

Keep a small bag ready, near the door, so you can grab it if you ever need to leave home quickly:

  • Water and food: at least 3 litres of water per person and dry food (biscuits, dates, dry fruit) for two days.
  • First aid: bandages, antiseptic, painkillers, and any regular medicine your family needs, with prescriptions.
  • Light and power: a torch with spare batteries, and a charged power bank for your phone.
  • Documents: photocopies of CNICs, a little cash in small notes, and a list of emergency numbers written on paper — Rescue 1122, the police helpline 15, and your family members' numbers.
  • Clothing and warmth: a warm shawl or blanket, and a change of clothes for children.
  • For children and babies: milk, diapers and a favourite toy — a calm child is easier to keep safe.
  • Basic tools: a whistle (to call for help if trapped), a small radio with batteries, and a dust mask.

Check the bag every six months: replace expired food and medicine, recharge the power bank, and update the numbers. A kit that is out of date is almost as useless as no kit at all. If you need a checklist to print, the free tools on Anwaar Free Tools can help you make one.

Make a family plan today

A plan made calmly today works when no one can think clearly. Sit with your family for ten minutes and agree on four things:

  • Safe spots in each room. Under the strongest table, against an inside wall away from windows. Everyone, including children, should know the safe spot in the room where they sleep.
  • A meeting point outside. Somewhere open and away from buildings — a park, a ground or a wide street — where the family gathers if the house must be left.
  • An out-of-town contact. When local networks jam, a call to a relative in another city often gets through. Agree that everyone reports to that one person.
  • What each person does. Who grabs the emergency bag, who checks on elderly parents, who switches off the gas if it is safe. Children should know one rule above all: drop, cover, hold on — and then find an adult.

Schools and workplaces should have the same plan, with regular drills. If your child's school has never practised an earthquake drill, ask the school to arrange one — the CyberSec Hub guides on spotting false information also apply here: during a disaster, a rumour shared carelessly can do real damage, and checking the source first is a habit worth teaching children too.

Myths and scams to ignore

  • "An earthquake is predicted for next Tuesday." False. Earthquakes cannot be predicted. Any message, video or "prediction" naming a date is a hoax — often copied and re-shared for years with the date changed.
  • "Animals behaving strangely means a big earthquake is coming." There is no reliable scientific evidence for this. Animals react to many things; their behaviour cannot forecast an earthquake.
  • "The weather was strange, so a quake is coming." Hot weather, clouds or a full moon do not cause earthquakes. Earthquakes come from movement deep underground, not from the sky.
  • "Send this message to 10 people and you will be safe." A chain message protects no one. The only protection is knowing what to do and having a plan.
  • Fake donation appeals after a disaster. After a real earthquake, scammers collect "donations" for victims through fake accounts and links. Donate only through known organisations and official channels, and never pay through a link sent by a stranger on WhatsApp.
  • Fake "live" earthquake videos. Old footage from other countries is often reposted as if it were today's earthquake in Pakistan. Check the PMD or USGS first — the real report will be there within minutes.

During a disaster, false information spreads faster than the truth. Before sharing any earthquake message, ask three questions: who published it, does it match an official source, and would I bet my family's safety on it? If the answer to any of these is unclear, do not share it. Frequently asked questions

How do I know if what I felt was an earthquake?

Check the USGS or EMSC map within a few minutes. If a tremor was recorded near your city at the time you felt it, that was it. Small tremors are sometimes felt by only a few people, and what feels like an earthquake can occasionally be a heavy truck, construction blasting or a sonic boom — the official map settles the question.

What is the difference between magnitude and intensity?

Magnitude is a single number for the earthquake itself — the energy released at the source. Intensity describes how strongly it was felt in a particular place, and it changes with distance: the same earthquake can be intensity VII near the epicentre and intensity III in a city far away.

Which areas of Pakistan are most at risk?

The northern areas — Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Gilgit-Baltistan, Azad Kashmir and the Hindu Kush region — and parts of Balochistan are the most earthquake-prone, because of the active fault lines there. But no part of Pakistan is free of risk, and even cities on the plains have felt strong tremors from distant epicentres. Everyone should know the safety steps, wherever they live.

Can earthquakes be predicted?

No. Scientists can say which areas are at risk over years and decades, but no one can predict the date, time, place and size of a specific earthquake. Early-warning systems can give a few seconds of warning after an earthquake has already started, but they cannot predict one before it happens.

Is my building safe?

Modern buildings built to proper seismic standards are far safer than old, unreinforced ones. If you live in an old building with visible cracks, a weak structure or unauthorised extra floors, get it checked by a qualified engineer. Reinforcing a building is expensive, but it is cheaper than rebuilding it — and it protects the people inside.

What should I do if I am trapped under rubble?

Stay calm, cover your mouth with cloth to keep out dust, and tap on a pipe or wall — or blow your whistle — at regular intervals so rescuers can hear you. Do not shout constantly, which uses up air and energy, and do not light a flame, which could ignite leaking gas.

The bottom line

Earthquakes in Pakistan are a fact of geography, not a matter of luck. You cannot predict them, but you can be ready for them: bookmark the PMD, USGS and EMSC pages today, switch on your phone's earthquake alerts, learn drop, cover and hold on, keep a small emergency bag by the door, and agree a family plan. When the next tremor comes, you will not need to ask what is happening — you will already know where to check, and what to do.
