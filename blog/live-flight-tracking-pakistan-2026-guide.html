Live Flight Tracking in Pakistan: How to Follow Any Flight in Real Time (2026 Guide)

What is live flight tracking?

Live flight tracking is the ability to see an aircraft's position, altitude, speed and route on a map while it is still in the air. Each plane on the map is a real aircraft, broadcasting its location several times a second. Tap a plane and you can see its flight number, where it took off, where it is going, how high it is flying and how fast it is moving.

Millions of people use flight tracking every day: families waiting at the airport, travellers checking whether their connecting flight will be delayed, aviation fans watching interesting routes, and businesses keeping track of cargo. In Pakistan, where many families have relatives working abroad in the Gulf, Europe and North America, it has become one of the most useful free tools on the internet — a quick look at the map tells you whether to leave for the airport now or wait another hour.

Flight tracking is not the same as booking a ticket. It does not sell flights and it does not change your booking. It simply shows you what is happening in the sky, right now, using data the aircraft itself broadcasts.

How does flight tracking actually work? (ADS-B explained)

Almost every modern passenger aircraft carries a small transmitter called an ADS-B transponder — short for Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast. Every second or so, it broadcasts the aircraft's GPS position, altitude, speed, heading and a unique 24-bit aircraft code on the 1090 MHz radio frequency. Anyone with a receiver within range can pick up these signals.

Around the world, thousands of volunteers and companies run small ADS-B receivers — some no bigger than a matchbox, plugged into a home internet connection. Together they form a global network that picks up these broadcasts and sends them to flight-tracking services, which draw the planes on a map. That is why a flight over the Atlantic, the Gulf or Punjab can appear on your screen within seconds of the aircraft broadcasting its position.

There are a few honest limits to know about. A plane only appears when at least one receiver is in range, so coverage is best near cities and busy air routes and thinner over oceans and remote areas. Very low-flying aircraft near the ground may not be picked up until they climb. And some military, government and private aircraft ask tracking services to hide their flights. For normal passenger flights in and out of Pakistan, though, coverage is very good — Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad all sit under busy air corridors with plenty of receivers nearby.

What information can you see about a flight?

Tap any aircraft on a live map and you will usually see:

  • Flight number and airline — for example, a Pakistan International Airlines (PIA) flight, an Emirates flight, or a Qatar Airways flight, with its route (Karachi to Dubai, Lahore to London, and so on).
  • Aircraft type and registration — the model (such as Airbus A320 or Boeing 777) and the tail number painted on the aircraft.
  • Altitude and speed — shown in feet and knots. A cruising airliner typically flies at 30,000–40,000 feet at around 450–550 knots (roughly 830–1,020 km/h). During take-off and landing these numbers drop fast, which is how you can tell a flight is about to land.
  • Heading and vertical speed — the direction the plane is flying and whether it is climbing or descending, and how fast.
  • Route, departure and arrival times — the planned route on the map, the scheduled and estimated arrival time, and how much of the journey is complete.
  • Position history — the trail the aircraft has already flown, which is the best way to spot a diversion: if the trail suddenly turns away from the destination airport, something has changed.

Tracking flights to and from Pakistan

Pakistan's three busiest international airports are Jinnah International, Karachi (KHI), Allama Iqbal International, Lahore (LHE) and Islamabad International Airport (ISB). Most international traffic from Pakistan flies west to the Gulf — Dubai, Doha, Abu Dhabi, Jeddah and Riyadh — or north and west to Europe and North America. These are also the routes families track most often.

To track a specific flight, you usually need only two things: the airline and the flight number, which are printed on the ticket (for example, "PK 785" or "EK 601"). Type the flight number into the search on a tracking map, or find the plane visually over Pakistan and tap it. If you do not know the flight number, search by route instead — "Karachi to Dubai" — and pick the departure time that matches.

A practical tip: check the flight about two hours before you plan to leave for the airport. If the incoming aircraft has not yet left its previous airport, your flight will almost certainly be delayed, because the same plane usually operates the next leg. And before driving to the airport, check the live weather forecast — fog in winter in Lahore and Islamabad, and monsoon rain in Karachi, are the most common weather reasons for delays in Pakistan.

Reading delays, diversions and holding patterns

A live map tells you more than the departure board does, if you know what to look for:

  • Delayed departure: the aircraft is still on the ground at the departure airport past its scheduled time, or the incoming aircraft has not arrived yet. The map shows the plane sitting at the gate, not moving.
  • Holding pattern: the aircraft is flying in circles or ovals near the destination airport instead of landing. This usually means the airport is busy, the weather is bad, or the runway is temporarily closed. A short hold of 10–20 minutes is normal; a long hold can mean a diversion is coming.
  • Diversion: the aircraft turns away from its destination and heads for another airport. On the map you will see the flight path bend towards a different city. Diversions happen for weather, medical emergencies or technical reasons — and the tracking map usually shows them before the airline announces them.
  • Go-around: the aircraft lines up to land, then climbs away again and circles for another try. You can see the altitude suddenly rise after dropping close to the ground. Go-arounds are a routine safety procedure, not a cause for panic.
  • Ground speed vs airspeed: the speed shown is usually ground speed — how fast the plane moves over the earth. A strong headwind can make a flight look slower even though the aircraft is flying normally, and a tailwind can make it look faster. That is also why the same route can take 20 minutes longer one day than the next.

Flight tracking apps vs the web

You can track flights in two ways: a dedicated app, or a website in your browser. Both use the same ADS-B data — the difference is convenience.

  • Apps can send push notifications when a flight takes off, lands or is delayed, and some let you point your phone at the sky to identify a plane overhead. The trade-off is storage space, battery use, and often a paid subscription for the best features.
  • Websites need nothing installed: open the page and the map is there. They work on any phone or computer, cost nothing, and are the fastest option when someone sends you a flight number on WhatsApp and you just want a quick look. The GlobalStream live flights map is built for exactly this — open it, tap a plane, done.

Whichever you use, the same advice applies: check the data's freshness. A good tracker shows when each position was received. If the "last update" is more than a minute or two old, the plane has moved on — trust the timestamp, not the dot.

Is flight tracking legal? And is it safe?

Yes. ADS-B signals are unencrypted radio broadcasts that aircraft transmit openly — the system was designed so that other aircraft and air-traffic control can see them. Receiving and displaying those broadcasts is legal in Pakistan and in most countries, and air-traffic control itself relies on the same data.

Tracking a flight does not reveal who is on board, and it cannot interfere with the aircraft in any way — receivers only listen; they never transmit to the plane. That said, be sensible about what you share: posting a screenshot of a flight you are on, in real time, tells the world exactly where you are. Many travellers wait until they have landed before sharing their journey. And if you use airport Wi-Fi to check your flight, keep your connection safe — never log in to your bank on an open network you do not trust.

Frequently asked questions

Can I track any flight in the world?

Almost any passenger flight with an ADS-B transponder — which is nearly all of them on international routes. Coverage is best over land and near cities; over oceans and remote regions a plane may disappear from the map for a while and reappear later.

Why does the map show my flight as delayed when the airline says on time?

Airlines report scheduled times; trackers show actual movement. If the plane is still at the gate, the tracker is showing the real situation. Trust the aircraft's actual position over the scheduled time — but also check the airline's own app, because only the airline knows about gate changes and rebooking.

How accurate is the arrival time shown on a tracker?

It is an estimate based on the aircraft's current speed, remaining distance and typical approach patterns. It gets more accurate as the flight gets closer to landing. Early in a long flight, the estimate can shift by 20 minutes or more if winds change.

Can I track cargo flights and private jets?

Cargo flights from major carriers usually appear just like passenger flights. Some private jets are blocked from public tracking at the owner's request, so they may not appear even though they are flying.
