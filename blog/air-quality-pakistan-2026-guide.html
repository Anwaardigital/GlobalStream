Air Quality in Pakistan: How to Read AQI and Protect Your Health (2026 Guide)

By Anwaar Digital · Last updated: 18 November 2026 · Health & daily life

Every winter, the air in Lahore turns grey, eyes start to sting, and the word on everyone's lips is "smog". In Karachi, the haze looks different — a dusty mix of traffic fumes and sea air. In Islamabad, the air is usually cleaner, but even there, some winter mornings arrive with a thick, still fog that smells of smoke.

Behind all of this is one number: the Air Quality Index, or AQI. It tells you, in a single figure, whether the air outside is safe to breathe — and what you should do about it. Yet most people in Pakistan have never been taught how to read it. This guide explains air quality in plain English: what AQI means, what is actually in the air, why Pakistan's air gets so bad in winter, how to check the live reading for your city, and — most importantly — how to protect yourself and your family. What is air quality, and what is AQI?

Air quality is a measure of how clean or dirty the air is — specifically, how much pollution is floating in it. The air we breathe is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, but in a city it also carries tiny particles and gases from vehicles, factories, burning fuel and dust. When those pollutants build up, the air becomes harmful, especially for children, older people, and anyone with asthma or heart problems.

The Air Quality Index (AQI) turns all of that into one simple number on a scale from 0 to 500. The lower the number, the cleaner the air. The higher the number, the more dangerous it is to breathe. It is the same scale used by air-quality monitors around the world, so a reading of 180 in Lahore means the same thing as a reading of 180 in Delhi or Beijing. That is what makes it so useful: one number, understood everywhere.

The AQI is calculated from several pollutants, and the reported number is set by whichever pollutant is worst at that moment. In Pakistan, that is almost always PM2.5 — the fine dust particles explained below. So when you see a high AQI in Lahore or Karachi, it usually means the PM2.5 level is high.

How to read the AQI scale

The AQI scale is divided into six colour-coded bands. Learn them once and you will never be confused by an air-quality number again:

AQILevelColourWhat it means
0–50GoodGreenAir quality is satisfactory. Enjoy normal outdoor activities.
51–100ModerateYellowAcceptable for most people. Unusually sensitive people should consider reducing long outdoor exercise.
101–150Unhealthy for Sensitive GroupsOrangeChildren, older adults and people with heart or lung disease should reduce long or heavy outdoor activity.
151–200UnhealthyRedEveryone may begin to feel health effects. Sensitive groups should avoid long outdoor activity; everyone else should reduce it.
201–300Very UnhealthyPurpleHealth alert: everyone is at risk of serious effects. Avoid outdoor activity.
301–500HazardousMaroonEmergency conditions. Everyone should stay indoors and avoid all outdoor physical activity.

A useful rule of thumb: below 100 is fine for most people, above 150 means take it seriously, and above 200 means stay indoors if you can. And remember — the AQI changes through the day. It is often worst in the early morning and late evening, when the air is still and cool, and better in the afternoon when the sun warms the air and it starts to move. That is why checking the live air quality matters more than remembering yesterday's number.

What is actually in the air? The main pollutants

AQI is one number, but it is built from several pollutants. Knowing them helps you understand where the bad air comes from — and what can fix it.

PM2.5 — the most dangerous one

PM2.5 means particles smaller than 2.5 micrometres — about 30 times thinner than a human hair. They come from vehicle exhaust, burning wood, crop residue and coal, brick kilns, and factories. Because they are so tiny, they pass deep into the lungs and even into the bloodstream. In Pakistan, PM2.5 is the pollutant that drives the AQI on most bad days, especially during the winter smog season in Punjab.

PM10 — coarse dust

PM10 particles are bigger — dust from roads, construction sites, deserts and dry fields. They irritate the eyes, nose and throat, and they make the air look hazy, but they do not go as deep into the lungs as PM2.5. Karachi and other dry, dusty cities often have high PM10 even when PM2.5 is moderate.

Ozone (O3)

Ground-level ozone forms when sunlight reacts with vehicle and factory fumes. It is worst on hot, sunny afternoons and irritates the lungs, especially during exercise. It is a summer problem more than a winter one.

Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and sulphur dioxide (SO2)

NO2 comes mainly from vehicle exhaust and power plants; SO2 comes from burning coal and furnace oil. Both irritate the airways and worsen asthma. High NO2 near a busy road is one reason the air feels worse at a traffic junction than in a park a few streets away.

Carbon monoxide (CO)

CO is a colourless, odourless gas from incomplete burning — old vehicles, generators and indoor stoves. Outdoors it is rarely the main problem, but indoors, a gas heater or generator running in a closed room can be deadly. Never run a generator or gas heater in a closed room.

Why does Pakistan's air get so bad — and why in winter?

Pakistan's air pollution is not one cause but several, stacking on top of each other. The main ones:

  • Winter smog in Punjab. From roughly November to January, cool, still air traps pollution close to the ground — a temperature inversion. Smoke from crop-residue burning, brick kilns, vehicles and factories has nowhere to go, so it builds up day after day. Lahore is the worst affected city, and the smog often spreads across the whole of Punjab, reaching Faisalabad, Gujranwala, Multan and Rawalpindi.
  • Vehicles. Millions of cars, motorcycles, rickshaws and trucks — many of them old, with poor maintenance and no emission checks — pour exhaust into city air every day. Diesel vehicles are a particularly big source of PM2.5.
  • Brick kilns and industry. Traditional brick kilns burning coal and old tyres are a major source of smoke around Lahore and other Punjab cities. Factories, especially those burning furnace oil, add more.
  • Crop-residue burning. After the rice harvest, farmers in Punjab burn leftover straw in their fields. The smoke drifts across the border region and into the cities, and it is one of the biggest contributors to the worst smog days.
  • Dust and construction. Dry soil, unpaved roads, construction sites and desert dust raise PM10, especially in Karachi, Quetta and the arid parts of Sindh and Balochistan.
  • Geography. Lahore sits on a flat plain with little wind in winter. Karachi's sea breeze helps clear the air, which is why its worst days are usually less extreme than Lahore's — though its average pollution is still high.

This is also why the weather forecast matters for air quality: wind, rain and temperature decide whether pollution clears or builds up. A rainy day washes the air clean; a still, foggy morning traps it. Check the live weather alongside the live air quality and you will start to see the pattern yourself.

Which cities are most affected?

Air quality varies a lot across Pakistan, and it changes with the season:

  • Lahore — the most affected major city, especially in winter. During the smog season it is frequently ranked among the most polluted cities in the world, and the AQI regularly crosses into the "Unhealthy" and "Very Unhealthy" bands. Schools have been closed on the worst days in recent years.
  • Karachi — pollution is high year-round from traffic, industry and dust, but the sea breeze usually keeps it below Lahore's winter peaks. The air is worst in dry, still weather and near busy roads and industrial areas.
  • Rawalpindi and Islamabad — Rawalpindi's traffic and dust make it noticeably worse than Islamabad, which benefits from the Margalla Hills and more green cover. Even Islamabad, though, gets smoggy days in winter.
  • Faisalabad, Gujranwala, Multan and Peshawar — all suffer from the same winter smog as Lahore, driven by industry, vehicles and crop burning. Peshawar's valley geography can trap pollution in winter.
  • Quetta and northern areas — usually cleaner, thanks to lower traffic and industry, but dust storms can push PM10 up sharply, and wood burning for heating adds PM2.5 in winter.

The lesson is simple: do not assume your city is fine because it is not Lahore. Check the live air quality for your city — the reading changes daily, and even within a city, the air near a busy road can be far worse than in a park.

How air pollution affects your health

Bad air is not just unpleasant — it is one of the biggest health risks in Pakistan, and it is easy to underestimate because the damage builds up slowly. Short-term exposure causes irritated eyes, a sore throat, coughing, headaches and tiredness. For people with asthma, it can trigger attacks. Longer exposure is linked to lung disease, heart disease and stroke, and children who grow up breathing polluted air can develop weaker lungs for life.

Some people are at much higher risk than others:

  • Children — they breathe faster than adults, their lungs are still developing, and they spend more time outdoors playing. Keep children indoors on high-AQI days and avoid outdoor school sports when the air is "Unhealthy" or worse.
  • Older people — heart and lung disease become more common with age, and pollution makes both worse.
  • People with asthma, COPD or heart disease — pollution can trigger attacks and hospital visits. Keep inhalers and medicines close on bad-air days.
  • Pregnant women — exposure to high PM2.5 during pregnancy is linked to poorer outcomes for the baby, so extra care matters.
  • Outdoor workers — traffic police, construction workers, rickshaw drivers and street vendors breathe the worst air for the longest hours. A proper mask is not a luxury for them; it is essential protection.

If you or a family member has a cough, wheezing or chest tightness that appears on smoggy days and improves when the air clears, that is a warning sign — talk to a doctor, do not just wait for winter to end. And if anyone has severe breathing difficulty, seek medical help immediately.

How to protect yourself and your family

You cannot clean the city's air by yourself — but you can cut your own exposure a lot. Here is what actually works:

1. Wear the right mask — and wear it properly

A loose cloth mask or a surgical mask does almost nothing against PM2.5. What works is a well-fitting N95 or KN95 mask, which filters at least 95% of fine particles — but only if it seals around your nose and mouth with no gaps. A beard or a loose fit breaks the seal. Wear one whenever the AQI is above 150 and you must go outside, and replace it when it becomes dirty or hard to breathe through. Keep a small stock at home before winter starts, because prices rise when smog arrives.

2. Keep indoor air cleaner than outdoor air

On bad days, keep windows and doors closed, especially in the early morning and late evening when pollution peaks. If you use an air purifier with a HEPA filter, run it in the room where you sleep. If you cannot afford one, a simple trick helps: keep one room — usually the bedroom — as clean as possible, with windows sealed and a damp cloth over gaps under doors. Do not burn wood, coal or mosquito coils indoors, and avoid smoking inside the house.

3. Time your outdoor activity

Exercise and outdoor work are healthiest when the air is cleanest — usually in the afternoon, when the sun has warmed the air and pollution has partly dispersed. Check the live AQI before a morning walk or jog; on a red or purple day, exercise indoors instead. Children should play indoors on high-AQI days, and school sports should move inside when the AQI passes 150.

4. Plan travel and prayer around the air

If you are driving, keep windows closed and use the car's air-conditioning on recirculation mode in heavy traffic — it filters some of the outside pollution. For the five daily prayers, check the prayer times and, on bad-air days, pray at home or in the nearest mosque rather than walking far through traffic. Before travelling between cities in winter, check the weather and the live flights too — smog causes flight delays and motorway closures, and a foggy morning on the motorway is dangerous.

5. Protect your eyes and throat

Simple habits help on smoggy days: wear glasses or sunglasses outdoors to shield your eyes, rinse your eyes with clean water after coming inside, drink plenty of water, and avoid rubbing irritated eyes. A saline nasal rinse can help if your nose feels blocked and dry.

6. Know when to stay home

When the AQI passes 200, the best protection is simply not going out. Work from home if you can, postpone non-essential trips, and keep children and older family members indoors. No errand is worth a hospital visit.

Your bad-air day plan (step by step)

When you wake up to grey, smoky air, follow this simple plan:

  • Check the number first. Open the GlobalStream air quality page and note the AQI for your city — not yesterday's, today's.
  • Decide by the band. Green or yellow: normal day. Orange: sensitive family members stay in. Red: everyone cuts outdoor time, children play indoors. Purple or maroon: stay indoors, masks on for any essential trip out.
  • Seal the house. Close windows, run the purifier or fan in the bedroom, and keep one clean room for sleeping.
  • Mask up for essentials. N95/KN95, sealed properly, for anyone who must go out.
  • Check the rest of the day. Look at the weather forecast — wind or rain will clear the air; a still, foggy forecast means it will get worse. If you have a flight, check live flights for delays before leaving for the airport.
  • Watch for symptoms. Coughing, wheezing, chest tightness or burning eyes that do not settle indoors are reasons to see a doctor.

Print this plan or save it on your phone before winter starts. On a bad-air morning, you will not want to work it out from scratch.

Frequently asked questions

What AQI level is safe to go outside?

Below 100 is fine for most people. Between 101 and 150, sensitive people — children, older adults, and anyone with asthma or heart disease — should reduce long outdoor activity. Above 150, everyone should cut outdoor time, and above 200, stay indoors if you can.

Is smog the same as fog?

No. Fog is just tiny water droplets in the air. Smog is fog mixed with smoke and pollution — that is why it smells, stings your eyes and lasts longer. If the air smells of smoke and your eyes burn, it is smog, and the AQI will confirm it.

Do surgical masks protect against air pollution?

Not really. Surgical masks and cloth masks block large droplets, but PM2.5 particles pass straight through them. Only a well-fitting N95 or KN95 mask filters fine particles properly.

Does rain clean the air?

Yes — rain washes particles and gases out of the air, which is why the AQI usually drops sharply after rain. Wind helps too. A still, dry, cold morning is the worst combination.

Why is Lahore's air worse than Karachi's?

Lahore suffers from winter temperature inversions that trap pollution, plus crop burning, brick kilns and heavy traffic on a flat plain with little wind. Karachi has plenty of pollution too, but its sea breeze usually disperses it better.

The bottom line

The AQI is a simple number with a serious message: below 100, enjoy your day; above 150, take it seriously; above 200, stay inside. Winter smog in Punjab, traffic, brick kilns and crop burning make Pakistan's air some of the most polluted in the world — but a daily habit of checking the live reading and acting on it protects your health more than any single purchase.
