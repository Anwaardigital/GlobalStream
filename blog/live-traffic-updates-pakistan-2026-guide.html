Live Traffic Updates in Pakistan: How to Check Road Conditions Before You Travel (2026 Guide)

By Anwaar Digital · Last updated: 23 November 2026 · Travel & live information

Every day, millions of people travel on Pakistan's roads — on motorways between cities, on the Grand Trunk Road, and through the crowded streets of Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad, Rawalpindi, Faisalabad, Multan and Peshawar. And every day, the same question is asked before people leave home: how is the traffic right now?

Road conditions in Pakistan change fast. A motorway can close in minutes when fog rolls in. A rainstorm can flood an underpass. A protest, an accident or roadworks can turn a 30-minute drive into a three-hour crawl. A route that was clear yesterday morning can be jammed today.

This guide explains, in plain English, how to check live traffic updates in Pakistan before you travel — using Google Maps, Waze, the Motorway Police, city traffic police pages, radio and news — and how to plan your journey around fog, rain and rush hours. It prints no traffic figures that will be out of date in an hour. Instead, it shows you where to check the live picture, every time you travel.

Why checking live traffic matters before you leave

Most of us check the weather before a journey. Traffic deserves the same habit — it decides how long the journey takes, how much fuel you burn, and whether you arrive on time at all.

A quick traffic check before you leave saves you from four common problems:

  • Getting stuck in a jam you could have avoided. A different route, or leaving 30 minutes earlier, often makes all the difference.
  • Driving into a closed motorway. In winter, motorways are often closed for hours because of fog. If you only find out at the toll plaza, you have wasted the whole trip to get there. Check before you leave home.
  • Missing a flight, a train, an exam or a wedding. If you are travelling to the airport or the railway station, the live traffic picture matters more than the distance. For onward air travel, check live flight tracking on GlobalStream the same way.
  • Running out of fuel in slow traffic. Stop-start traffic burns far more fuel than a smooth run. Knowing the road is jammed helps you fill up first — and check the live petrol price before you do.

The rule is simple: check the route when you plan, and check the live traffic again just before you leave. Conditions change while you are getting ready.

1. Google Maps: the first thing to check

For most people in Pakistan, Google Maps is the best single place to check live traffic. It is free, it works on almost every phone, and it covers motorways, highways and city streets across the country.

Here is how to use it properly:

  • Turn on the traffic layer. Open Google Maps, tap the layers button (the small square icon), and switch on "Traffic". Roads are coloured green (moving well), orange (slow), red (heavy traffic) and dark red (standstill). This is the fastest way to see the whole city at a glance.
  • Search your route before you leave. Enter your destination and look at the estimated travel time. Google Maps shows the time with current traffic — not the time on an empty road. If the time suddenly jumps by 20 or 30 minutes, something has happened on your usual route.
  • Compare the suggested routes. Google Maps usually offers two or three routes, with a time for each. The fastest one is not always the shortest — it may use a motorway or a bypass to go around a jam.
  • Check the arrival time, not just the distance. Two routes can be the same distance but 40 minutes apart in traffic. Always read the minutes, not the kilometres.
  • Read the incident icons. Google Maps shows accidents, road closures, roadworks and police checks reported by other drivers. Tap an icon to see what happened and how long ago it was reported.
  • Check again before you leave. A route that was green at 8 in the morning can be red by 9. Check the map one more time as you walk to the car.

Google Maps traffic works best where many people are driving with their phones on — which is why it is most accurate in big cities and on motorways. On quiet rural roads, there may be less live data, so treat the colours as a guide and drive carefully. And never look at the map while driving — set the route before you start, and let the voice directions guide you.

2. Waze and other navigation apps

Waze is another free navigation app, built entirely around live reports from drivers. It is less widely used in Pakistan than Google Maps, but in the cities where it has users it can be very useful, because its alerts come from people on the same road minutes earlier.

Waze is good for:

  • Live hazard alerts — accidents, broken-down vehicles, objects on the road and police checks, reported by drivers ahead of you.
  • Road closure reports — when a road is blocked, Waze users report it quickly, and the app routes you around it.
  • Real speed and delay information — because Waze users share their speed, the app knows how slow a stretch really is.

Other useful apps include the navigation built into many phones, and ride-hailing apps, which show live traffic on their maps when you book a ride — a quick way to see if your area is jammed before you even open a map app. Whichever app you use, the habit is the same: check before you leave, and check the live picture, not a printed map.

3. National Highway & Motorway Police: the official source for motorways

For travel on motorways and national highways, the most reliable source is the National Highway & Motorway Police (NHMP) — the force that patrols the motorways. When a motorway is closed, diverted or restricted, the NHMP is the authority that announces it.

How to get NHMP updates:

  • Call the NHMP helpline, 130. This is the Motorway Police helpline. Before a long journey, call and ask about the condition of the motorway you plan to use — especially in winter fog season or after heavy rain. Keep your route and departure time ready when you call.
  • Follow the NHMP's official social media pages. The Motorway Police post closure and reopening notices, fog advisories and accident alerts on their official Facebook and X (Twitter) pages. Check them before a motorway journey, and check again on the morning of travel.
  • Listen to the announcements at toll plazas and service areas. When a motorway is closed, the staff at the entry points will tell you. But it is far better to know before you drive there — a call or a quick check online saves a wasted trip.
  • Watch for fog advisories in winter. From December to February, thick fog across Punjab often closes motorways overnight and in the early morning. The NHMP announces closures section by section — one stretch may be open while the next is closed. Always check the exact section you plan to drive, not just the motorway's name. Check the weather forecast the night before as well.

Never trust a WhatsApp forward that claims a motorway is closed. Old closure notices are forwarded again and again, sometimes months after the road reopened. Only the NHMP's own pages and helpline count — check the date and time on any notice before you believe it.

4. City traffic police: updates for Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad and other cities

Inside the big cities, the city traffic police are the best source for road closures, diversions and protest routes. Most city traffic police departments now run official Facebook and X (Twitter) pages where they post:

  • Road closures and diversions — for construction, VIP movement, processions, cricket matches and public events.
  • Protest and rally routes — so you can avoid the affected roads in advance.
  • One-way and parking changes — temporary changes around markets, stadiums and shrines during festivals.
  • Accident alerts — major accidents that block a main road.

Before driving across a big city, open the traffic police page for that city and look at the latest posts. In Lahore, Karachi and Islamabad, the traffic police also run FM radio updates during rush hours, and local TV news channels carry a traffic segment in the morning and evening. If your journey passes through a city centre, the traffic police page is worth more than a map app alone — a map shows you the jam, but the police page tells you why it is there and how long it is likely to last.

Also check the city traffic police pages before travelling on public holidays and during Eid, Muharram processions and cricket matches, when whole roads can be closed for hours. For nationwide news that affects travel — protests, strikes, weather emergencies — check the Pakistan news page and the world news page on GlobalStream.

5. Radio, TV and news: the old ways still work

Apps are not the only source of live traffic information — and on the road, they are not always the most practical one.

  • FM radio. Most cities have FM stations that give traffic updates during the morning and evening rush. If you are already driving, the radio is the safest way to hear about a jam ahead — keep your eyes on the road and let the radio do the checking.
  • TV news tickers. Morning news shows on Pakistani TV channels often run a traffic segment, and breaking news tickers announce major closures and accidents as they happen.
  • News websites and apps. When a major road is blocked for hours — a protest, a flood, a serious accident — it becomes news within minutes. A quick look at a news app before you leave can warn you about something no map app has picked up yet.

The best approach is to combine sources: a map app for the live picture, the traffic police page for the reason, and the radio while you drive. No single source is perfect, but together they give you a clear picture.

6. Fog and smog season: winter driving in Pakistan

From December to February, fog is the biggest traffic problem in Pakistan — and the most dangerous. Thick fog covers the motorways and highways of Punjab, especially between Lahore, Islamabad, Gujranwala, Sialkot, Faisalabad and Multan. Visibility can drop to almost nothing in minutes.

How to travel safely in fog season:

  • Check the NHMP before every motorway journey. Motorways are closed when visibility becomes dangerous, and they reopen only when it improves. Call 130 or check the NHMP pages — do not guess.
  • Check the weather forecast the night before. The GlobalStream weather page shows the forecast for your city and your destination, including fog warnings.
  • Avoid travelling at night and in the early morning. Fog is thickest between midnight and mid-morning. If you can, travel after 11 in the morning, when the fog usually lifts.
  • Use fog lights and drive slowly. If you are caught in fog, switch on your fog lights (not your high beam — it reflects back and makes things worse), slow down, keep a long distance from the vehicle ahead, and use the road markings to guide you.
  • Do not stop on the motorway in fog. If you must stop, pull completely off the road into a service area. A stopped vehicle in thick fog is extremely dangerous.
  • Watch out for smog in Lahore and other cities. In winter, smog can reduce visibility in cities almost as badly as fog. The air quality page shows current conditions, and on the worst days it is better to delay non-essential travel.

If the motorway is closed, do not try alternative back roads in thick fog — they are narrower, unlit and often more dangerous than the motorway. Wait, or take the train instead. The guide to Pakistan Railways live train timings explains how to check trains when the roads are closed.

7. Monsoon, rain and floods: summer driving

From July to September, the monsoon brings a different problem: heavy rain, flooded underpasses and waterlogged roads. Low-lying areas in Karachi, Lahore and Rawalpindi can flood within an hour of heavy rain, and mountain roads in the north can be blocked by landslides.

Before travelling in the monsoon:

  • Check the weather forecast and the rain radar for your route, not just your city. Rain 50 kilometres away can flood the road you are about to drive on.
  • Never drive through flooded water. You cannot tell how deep it is, and a few inches of fast-moving water can carry a car away. Turn around — it is never worth the risk.
  • Avoid underpasses in heavy rain. Underpasses are the first places to flood in Pakistani cities. If it is raining hard, take the overhead bridge or a higher road instead.
  • Check mountain road conditions before heading north. In the northern areas, rain can cause landslides that close the Karakoram Highway and other mountain roads for hours or days. Check local news and the district administration's announcements before you set off.

8. The best times to travel — and the worst

Traffic in Pakistani cities follows a pattern. Knowing it helps you plan even when you cannot check a map:

  • Morning rush: roughly 8 to 10 in the morning. School and office traffic fills the main roads. Leave earlier or later if you can.
  • Midday: usually the quietest time. Between late morning and mid-afternoon, roads are often at their clearest — the best window for a cross-city journey.
  • Evening rush: roughly 5 to 8 in the evening. The return journey home, combined with shopping and market traffic, makes this the worst time to drive across a city.
  • Friday afternoons: roads around mosques and markets are busy at prayer time, and many offices close early.
  • Weekends and public holidays: motorways out of big cities are busy on Friday evenings and Saturday mornings, and busy coming back on Sunday evenings.
  • Ramadan evenings: traffic peaks sharply just before Iftar, and again after Taraweeh prayers. Plan around these times — and check the prayer times for your city when you plan a Ramadan journey.

For intercity travel, the smoothest journeys are usually in the late morning, after the fog has lifted and before the evening rush. If you must travel at a busy time, leave early and build in an extra hour — arriving early is always better than arriving late.

9. Planning the route: a simple routine for every journey

Put the checks together into a routine you can follow every time:

  • The night before: check the weather forecast for your route and your destination, and check whether your motorway section is open. If you are travelling in winter or the monsoon, this step matters most.
  • One hour before leaving: open Google Maps and look at the traffic colours and the travel time for your route. Check the traffic police page for your city and the NHMP pages if you are using a motorway.
  • Just before you leave: check the map one more time. If the time has jumped, consider a different route or a later departure.
  • On the road: let the voice navigation guide you, listen to FM radio for updates, and do not touch your phone while driving. If traffic gets worse, pull over safely before checking the map again.
  • Carry the essentials: a charged phone, a power bank, drinking water, and enough fuel. In slow traffic, a phone with a dead battery is a real problem — your map, your contacts and your payment apps all live on it.

If you are comparing the cost of driving with the train or a flight, check the petrol price, the train timings guide and the live flights page before you decide. And if you are carrying foreign currency for the trip, the currency rates page shows the live rupee rate.

10. Staying safe on the road: scams and driving habits

  • Do not believe forwarded "road closed" messages. Old closure notices circulate on WhatsApp for months. Check the NHMP or the city traffic police pages — and the date on the notice — before changing your plans.
  • Do not follow a stranger's shortcut blindly. In a jam, some drivers follow others down side streets that lead nowhere. Trust your map, not the car in front.
  • Keep your documents with you. Carry your driving licence, vehicle registration and CNIC. Traffic police check documents at checkpoints, especially on motorways.
  • Wear your seatbelt and keep to the speed limit. Most serious accidents in Pakistan involve speeding or overtaking on single-carriageway roads. Arriving ten minutes late is better than not arriving at all.
  • Do not drive when you are tired. On long journeys, stop every two hours at a service area, rest, and drink water. Tired driving is as dangerous as fast driving.
  • Keep your phone charged and your fuel tank above a quarter. In a long jam or a motorway closure, a full tank and a charged phone are the difference between an inconvenience and a real problem.

Frequently asked questions

How can I check live traffic in Pakistan?

Open Google Maps and switch on the traffic layer, or use Waze for driver-reported alerts. For motorways, call the Motorway Police helpline 130 or check the NHMP's official pages. For city roads, check your city traffic police's official Facebook or X page, and listen to FM radio while you drive.

How do I know if a motorway is closed?

Call the NHMP helpline, 130, or check the Motorway Police's official social media pages. Motorways are closed section by section, so ask about the exact stretch you plan to drive — one section can be open while the next is closed.

Why is Google Maps showing red on my route?

Red means heavy traffic and dark red means near-standstill. Tap the road to see the cause — an accident, roadworks, a closure or simply rush-hour volume — and look at the alternative routes Google Maps suggests, with their travel times.

What is the best time to travel between cities in Pakistan?

Late morning is usually smoothest — after the winter fog lifts and before the evening rush. Avoid the early morning in fog season, Friday evenings out of big cities, and Sunday evenings coming back.

Is it safe to drive in fog on the motorway?

No — thick fog is one of the most dangerous conditions on Pakistani roads. If the NHMP has closed the motorway, do not try back roads instead. Wait for the fog to lift, travel later in the day, or take the train.

How do I check traffic during a protest or a cricket match?

Check your city traffic police's official page before you leave — they post closures and diversion routes for protests, processions and matches. Local TV news and FM radio also carry these updates, and the Pakistan news page covers major events.

What should I do if I get stuck in a traffic jam?

Stay calm, keep your doors locked, and do not leave your car in an unknown area at night. Let your family know you are delayed, check the map only when safely parked, and wait for the road to clear rather than forcing your way through side streets you do not know.

The bottom line

Pakistan's roads reward the traveller who checks before leaving. A two-minute look at Google Maps, a quick call to the Motorway Police helpline 130, and a glance at your city traffic police page can save you hours — and keep you safe in fog, rain and rush hour.

Remember the routine: check the weather and the motorway the night before, check the live map an hour before you leave, and check once more as you walk to the car. Traffic changes fast in Pakistan — the live picture is the only one that counts.
