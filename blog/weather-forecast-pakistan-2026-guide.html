Weather Forecast in Pakistan: How to Read It and Plan Your Day (2026 Guide)

By Anwaar Digital · Last updated: 15 November 2026 · Weather & daily life

Every morning, millions of people in Pakistan do the same thing before leaving home: they check the weather. Will it rain on the way to work? Is today a day for a light shirt or a warm shawl? Can the children play outside, or should the washing stay indoors? The forecast answers all of these questions — if you know how to read it.

This guide explains the weather forecast in Pakistan in plain English: what each number means, how the seasons and regions differ, how to plan your day, travel and farm work around the forecast, and how to stay safe in monsoon rain, heatwaves and winter fog. There are no printed temperatures in this article — the weather changes every hour, so the numbers belong on the live page. Instead, this guide teaches you how to read those numbers, and shows you where to check them live for free on GlobalStream's weather page.

The numbers on a forecast, explained

A forecast is not one number. It is a small set of numbers, and each one answers a different question. Here is what they mean.

Temperature — and "feels like"

The temperature is the air temperature measured in the shade, in degrees Celsius. The "feels like" temperature is what the air actually feels like on your skin, once wind and humidity are taken into account. On a humid day in Karachi, 34 degrees can feel like 42. On a windy winter morning in Islamabad, 8 degrees can feel like 4. When you decide what to wear, trust the "feels like" number more than the plain temperature.

Humidity

Humidity is how much water vapour the air holds, shown as a percentage. High humidity — common on the coast and during the monsoon — stops sweat from drying, so you feel hotter and more tired. Low humidity — common in Balochistan and the dry winter months — dries the skin and throat, and makes the air feel cooler than the thermometer says. If the humidity is high, drink extra water even if you do not feel thirsty.

Wind speed and direction

Wind is shown as a speed (kilometres per hour) and a direction (for example, "wind from the west"). Wind cools you down in summer, makes cold feel colder in winter, and carries dust and smoke. Strong wind also matters for travel: it can delay flights, make motorbike rides difficult, and blow loose roofs and signboards around. Check the wind before a long drive, and before booking a flight on the live flights page.

Chance of rain

The "chance of rain" is a probability, not a promise. A 70% chance of rain means that, in similar weather conditions, rain fell 7 times out of 10 — not that it will rain for 70% of the day. A 20% chance still means rain is possible, just unlikely. Also check the expected amount of rain: a light shower and a heavy downpour both count as "rain", but they need very different plans.

UV index

The UV index measures how strong the sun's ultraviolet rays are. A high UV index means sunburn comes fast, even on a cloudy day. In the Pakistani summer the UV index is often high by late morning. If you work outdoors, wear a hat, cover your arms, and stay in the shade in the middle of the day.

Visibility and pressure

Visibility tells you how far you can see clearly — important in fog and dust storms. Air pressure, shown in hectopascals, usually falls before a storm and rises in clear, settled weather. You do not need to memorise pressure values; just know that a sudden fall often means the weather is about to change.

Pakistan's seasons — a plain guide

Pakistan has four clear seasons, but they do not arrive on the same dates everywhere. As a rough guide for the plains:

  • Winter (December to February). Cool to cold, especially at night and in the north. The plains can be foggy in the morning. This is the season for warm clothes, heaters, and slow, careful driving in fog.
  • Spring (March to April). The most pleasant season in most of the country. Temperatures are mild, flowers bloom, and it is the best time for outdoor events and travel.
  • Summer (May to June). Very hot in the plains, particularly in interior Sindh and southern Punjab. This is heatwave season — plan outdoor work for early morning or evening, and drink plenty of water.
  • Monsoon (July to September). The rainy season. The monsoon brings heavy rain, especially in the north and east, and can cause urban flooding in Karachi and Lahore. Check the forecast daily in this season — a single heavy spell can change a whole day's plans.
  • Autumn (October to November). Temperatures fall, the air dries out, and the weather becomes pleasant again. A good season for travel and outdoor work before winter fog arrives.

The mountains follow a different calendar: winter there is long and snowy, and summer is short and mild. The coast around Karachi has a smaller range between seasons — never as cold as the north in winter, never as dry-hot as the interior in summer, but humid for much of the year.

One country, many climates

Pakistan is not one climate. It is several, side by side — and a forecast that matters in one city may mean nothing in another.

  • Karachi and the coast. The sea breeze keeps temperatures steadier, but humidity is high for much of the year. Rain is less frequent but can be very heavy when it comes, and the city's drains can overflow quickly. Sea breeze also means evenings are usually cooler than the afternoon.
  • Lahore and central Punjab. Hot summers, cold foggy winters, and the monsoon in between. Winter mornings often bring dense fog that closes motorways and delays flights — always check the fog and the flight status before travelling in December and January.
  • Islamabad and the Potohar plateau. Milder than the plains, with more rain through the year. Summer evenings are pleasant, and winter brings cold nights. Rain can arrive suddenly from the Margalla hills.
  • The northern areas — Gilgit-Baltistan, Chitral, and the mountains of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Cold most of the year, with snow in winter. Even in summer, nights are cool. Mountain weather changes fast: a sunny morning can turn into rain or snow by afternoon, and landslides can block roads after heavy rain. Travellers should check the forecast every morning and keep a day of flexibility in their plans.
  • Balochistan. Mostly dry, with big differences between day and night temperatures. Quetta has cold winters, while the southern districts stay warm. Dust and dry wind are common, and rain is rare but can be intense.
  • Interior Sindh and the Thar desert. Among the hottest and driest parts of the country in summer, with cold desert nights in winter. Water is precious here, and the forecast matters most for farmers deciding when to sow and when to expect the monsoon.

That is why a national forecast is only a starting point. Always check the forecast for your own city — the GlobalStream weather page covers cities across all provinces.

Planning your day around the forecast

A forecast is only useful if it changes what you do. Here is how to use it:

  • Clothes. Check the morning low and the afternoon high, not just one number. Dress for the "feels like" temperature, and carry a light layer if the day will swing between warm and cool.
  • Travel and commute. Rain slows traffic everywhere. Leave earlier on a rainy day, and check the forecast before a motorway trip — fog can close the motorway with little warning. If you are flying, check the live flights page as well as the weather at both ends of the journey.
  • School and children. On very hot days, send children with extra water. On foggy mornings, leave earlier — school vans move slowly in fog, and visibility near the road is poor.
  • Outdoor events. Weddings, cricket matches and family gatherings are often planned outdoors. Check the forecast a few days ahead, and again on the morning of the event. A tent or a covered hall is cheap insurance in monsoon season.
  • Household work. Laundry dries slowly on humid days and fast on dry, windy days. Plan washing for a dry, sunny stretch. In the monsoon, dry clothes indoors where the rain cannot reach them.
  • Health. On hot days, plan exercise for early morning or after sunset. People with asthma or breathing problems should also check the air quality page — smog and dust make breathing harder even when the temperature looks fine.

For farmers, shopkeepers and travellers

The forecast is a working tool, not just a curiosity. Farmers use it to decide when to sow, when to irrigate, and when to harvest — cutting a crop just before heavy rain can save it, and cutting it just after can ruin it. Shopkeepers watch the rain forecast to plan stock: umbrellas, raincoats and plastic sheeting sell when the monsoon arrives, and cold drinks sell when the heat rises. Transporters check the forecast before sending trucks over the mountains, where a single landslide can close a road for a day.

Travellers gain the most from the forecast. Before a trip inside Pakistan, check the weather for every city on your route, not just your destination — the mountains between Islamabad and Gilgit can be snowing while the plains are sunny. Before a flight, check the weather at both airports: fog in Lahore can delay a morning departure, and a storm at the destination can delay the landing. Staying safe: monsoon rain, heatwaves and winter fog

Most weather in Pakistan is harmless. Three kinds of weather are not, and each has simple rules.

Monsoon rain and flooding

  • Never drive or walk through flooded roads or underpasses — the water is often deeper than it looks, and it can hide open manholes.
  • Stay away from electricity poles and fallen wires in the rain.
  • If lightning is near, go indoors and stay away from windows and metal objects.
  • Keep important documents and medicines in a waterproof bag during the monsoon.

Heatwaves

  • Drink water regularly, even before you feel thirsty. Avoid very cold drinks after long exposure to heat.
  • Stay out of direct sun between late morning and mid-afternoon.
  • Rest in the shade if you feel dizzy, sick or confused — those are warning signs of heatstroke, and you should get to a cool place and seek medical help.
  • Check on elderly people and small children first; they suffer from heat sooner than healthy adults.

Winter fog and cold

  • In dense fog, drive slowly, keep your lights on low beam, and keep a long distance from the vehicle ahead. If you cannot see, pull over safely and wait.
  • Dress in layers — several thin layers keep you warmer than one thick one.
  • Keep children and elderly people warm at night, and be careful with gas heaters: never sleep with a gas heater on in a closed room.

When to check, and why forecasts change

Forecasts are made by computer models that take in millions of measurements — from satellites, weather stations, ships and aircraft — and calculate how the atmosphere will move. The models are good, but the atmosphere is complicated, so forecasts are not perfect. A few rules help you use them well:

  • Check in the morning and again in the evening. The morning check sets your day; the evening check catches changes for tomorrow.
  • Trust the next 24 hours most. Short-range forecasts are the most reliable. A forecast for tomorrow is usually good; a forecast for next week is a rough guide, not a promise.
  • Expect changes in the monsoon. Monsoon rain depends on small shifts in wind and moisture, so the forecast can change several times a day. That is normal, not a mistake.
  • Compare the forecast with the sky. If the forecast says clear skies but dark clouds are building over your city, trust your eyes and take an umbrella. Forecasts describe a whole area; your street can differ.
  • Use the same source every day. Different forecast models can disagree. Pick one reliable source — such as the GlobalStream weather page — and learn how it behaves for your city, instead of jumping between sources.

Common mistakes when reading a forecast

  • Reading only the high temperature. The low, the "feels like", the rain chance and the wind matter just as much.
  • Trusting a 10-day forecast like a timetable. Treat long-range forecasts as a trend, not a schedule.
  • Ignoring the rain chance. A 30% chance of rain still means rain is possible — carry an umbrella if the day matters.
  • Forgetting your own city. A forecast for "Pakistan" or even for "Punjab" is not a forecast for your street. Always check your city.
  • Checking once and never again. Weather moves. A forecast checked yesterday morning is not today's forecast.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the forecast keep changing?

Because new measurements arrive all the time, and the computer models update their calculations with each new set. A forecast is a best estimate, and it improves as the time gets closer. The forecast for today is far more reliable than the forecast for next week.

What does a 50% chance of rain mean?

It means that in similar conditions, rain fell about half the time. It does not mean it will rain for half the day, and it does not mean it will rain on half of the city. Treat it as "take an umbrella if you will be out".

Why is it hotter in Karachi than the temperature says?

Humidity. Karachi's sea air holds a lot of moisture, which stops sweat from drying and makes the same temperature feel much hotter. Check the "feels like" temperature — that is the number your body actually experiences.

Why is there fog in Lahore but not in Karachi?

Fog forms when moist air cools to the point where water droplets form, usually on cold, still winter nights. Lahore's winter nights are cold and calm, which is ideal for fog. Karachi's sea breeze keeps the air moving and warmer, so fog is rare there.

Which is the best time of year to travel in Pakistan?

Spring and autumn are the most comfortable for most of the country. The northern areas are best in late spring and summer, when the roads are open and the weather is mild. Winter travel in the mountains is beautiful but can be blocked by snow, so always check the forecast and road conditions first.

The bottom line

A weather forecast is a daily tool: temperature and "feels like" tell you what to wear, the rain chance tells you what to carry, and the wind and humidity tell you how the day will actually feel. Pakistan's weather changes a lot from city to city and season to season — the coast, the plains, the mountains and the desert each have their own rules — so always check your own city's forecast, check it twice a day in the monsoon, and respect the three dangers: heavy rain, heatwaves and fog.
