Currency Exchange Rates in Pakistan: How to Read Them and Get the Best Rate (2026 Guide)

By Anwaar Digital · Last updated: 11 November 2026 · Money & currency

Every day, millions of Pakistanis check the dollar rate — overseas workers sending money home, families paying university fees abroad, shopkeepers importing goods, and travellers buying foreign currency before a trip. Yet the exchange rate is one of the most misunderstood numbers in the country. Why do a bank, a money changer and a website sometimes show three different rates for the same dollar on the same day? What is the difference between the interbank rate and the open market rate? And how can you tell whether the rate you are being offered is fair?

This guide explains currency exchange rates in Pakistan in plain English: what a rate is, how to read a rate table, what moves the rupee, how to get the best rate when you send or receive money, and how to avoid the scams that target people exchanging currency. All live rates mentioned here can be checked free on GlobalStream's currency page — this article deliberately gives you no rate numbers of its own, because rates change by the minute and a printed number would be out of date before you read it.

What is an exchange rate?

An exchange rate is simply the price of one currency in terms of another. When you see "USD/PKR", it tells you how many Pakistani rupees one US dollar buys. If the rate goes up, the rupee has weakened — each dollar costs more rupees. If the rate goes down, the rupee has strengthened — each dollar costs fewer rupees.

Exchange rates are quoted as a pair, and the order matters. "USD/PKR" and "PKR/USD" are two different numbers. In Pakistan, rates are almost always quoted as rupees per foreign currency — rupees per dollar, rupees per pound, rupees per Saudi riyal. When you see a table of rates, the first thing to check is which way round the pair is quoted, because mixing them up is the most common beginner mistake.

Rates also move constantly during the day. Unlike the price of bread, which changes rarely, currency prices move every minute while markets are open. A rate you saw in the morning may be different by the evening. That is why any serious guide — including this one — points you to a live rate page instead of printing a number. Interbank rate vs open market rate: the two rates that matter in Pakistan

In Pakistan you will hear two different rates quoted for the same currency, and understanding the difference is the key to not being overcharged.

The interbank rate

The interbank rate is the rate at which banks trade large amounts of currency with each other. It is the "wholesale" rate, and it is the number you will see in news headlines and on the State Bank of Pakistan's website. It is set by supply and demand in the banking system and is usually the best rate available — but ordinary people cannot normally exchange money at this rate. It is a benchmark, not a shop price.

The open market rate

The open market rate is the rate charged by licensed money changers and exchange companies — the shops you see in every city's main bazaar. This is the rate that matters when you walk in with cash. It is almost always a little worse than the interbank rate, because the exchange company has costs to cover and a profit to make. The gap between the two rates is usually small when the market is calm and wider when the market is nervous. If the gap suddenly becomes very large, it is a sign of stress in the currency market — and a reason to be extra careful about where you exchange.

Why two shops quote two prices

Different exchange companies have different costs, different stocks of currency, and different ideas about where the market is heading. A shop that is short of dollars will offer a worse rate to buy dollars and a better rate to sell them. Rates also differ between cities and even between streets. Comparing two or three licensed money changers before a large exchange is one of the simplest ways to save money — the difference on a large amount can be thousands of rupees.

How to read a rate table

A rate table looks confusing the first time, but it has only a few moving parts. Once you know them, you can read any table in seconds.

  • Buying rate and selling rate. Every currency has two prices. The buying rate is what the bank or money changer pays you when you sell them foreign currency. The selling rate is what you pay them when you buy foreign currency from them. The selling rate is always higher than the buying rate — that gap is called the spread, and it is how the dealer earns money. Always check which column you are reading: if you are selling dollars, you want the highest buying rate; if you are buying dollars, you want the lowest selling rate.
  • The spread. A narrow spread means a competitive dealer; a wide spread means you are paying a lot for the service. Compare spreads, not just headline rates — a dealer who advertises a great selling rate but a terrible buying rate is not giving you a good deal overall.
  • Currency codes. Rates are shown with three-letter codes: USD for the US dollar, GBP for the British pound, EUR for the euro, AED for the UAE dirham, SAR for the Saudi riyal. These codes are international, so the same code means the same currency everywhere in the world.
  • Cash vs transfer rates. Some tables show different rates for cash and for bank transfers. Transfer rates are usually slightly better, because handling cash costs the dealer money. If you are sending money by bank transfer, look for the transfer rate, not the cash rate.
  • Last-updated time. A rate without a time stamp is not trustworthy. The GlobalStream currency page shows the last-updated time next to every rate, so you know whether the number you are looking at is minutes old or hours old.

One practical example: if you are an overseas Pakistani sending dollars home, you are selling dollars, so you want the dealer with the highest buying rate. If you are a traveller buying dollars for a trip, you are buying, so you want the lowest selling rate. Getting this the wrong way round is the single most expensive beginner mistake.

What moves the rupee?

The rupee does not move randomly. Several forces push it up or down, and most of them are visible in the news if you know what to look for.

1. Imports and exports

Pakistan imports far more than it exports — fuel, machinery, food and medicines are all bought in dollars. When import bills rise, demand for dollars rises and the rupee usually weakens. When exports grow, more dollars flow in and the rupee is supported. That is why news about the trade deficit is really news about the rupee.

2. Remittances from overseas Pakistanis

Money sent home by Pakistanis working abroad is one of the country's biggest sources of dollars. When remittances are strong, the rupee is supported; when they fall, the rupee comes under pressure. If you are one of the millions of overseas Pakistanis sending money home, your transfer is part of this story — and the rate you get matters twice, to your family and to the country.

3. Foreign reserves and the State Bank

The State Bank of Pakistan holds dollars in reserve and can buy or sell them to smooth out sharp moves. Large reserves give the central bank room to steady the market; low reserves mean the rupee is more exposed to sudden swings. Reserve figures are published regularly and are widely reported in Pakistan news.

4. Interest rates and inflation

When interest rates in Pakistan are high compared with other countries, foreign investors are more willing to hold rupees, which supports the currency. When inflation is high and rising, the rupee tends to weaken, because each rupee buys less over time. These are slow-moving forces — they shape the trend over months, not the rate on a single day.

5. The global dollar

Sometimes the rupee moves not because of anything in Pakistan, but because the dollar itself is strengthening or weakening against every currency in the world. When the US central bank raises interest rates, the dollar usually rises globally, and the rupee — like most currencies — falls against it. The world news page on GlobalStream is a good place to follow these global moves.

6. Politics, confidence and the gold price

Currency markets run on confidence. Political uncertainty, delayed decisions on loans, or worries about the economy can push people to buy dollars as a safe haven, weakening the rupee. Gold often moves at the same time, for the same reason — when confidence falls, people buy both dollars and gold. Where to check live exchange rates

Never trust a rate from a social media post, a forwarded WhatsApp message or a screenshot — these are often old, wrong or deliberately misleading. Check a live source at the moment you need the rate:

  • Open the GlobalStream currency page for live rates against the rupee, with the last-updated time shown for every currency.
  • Cross-check with your own bank's published rates or the State Bank of Pakistan's daily rates for the interbank benchmark.
  • For cash rates, call or visit two or three licensed money changers and compare their buying and selling rates — and check the spread.
  • Ignore rates quoted by unlicensed dealers on the street or in chat groups. A rate that looks far better than every licensed dealer is not a bargain; it is a warning sign.

Sending money to Pakistan: remittance tips

For overseas Pakistanis, the exchange rate directly decides how many rupees your family receives. A small difference in the rate, repeated every month, adds up to a large amount over a year.

  • Use official channels. Send money through a bank or a licensed money transfer company, never through informal channels. Official transfers are protected, recorded, and often rewarded — Pakistan's remittance schemes have at times offered incentives for using legal channels, and your family receives the money safely.
  • Compare the rate, not just the fee. A service that advertises "zero fee" may hide its cost in a poor exchange rate. Add the fee and the rate difference together to find the real cost: a slightly higher fee with a much better rate is usually the cheaper option.
  • Ask whether the rate is locked. Some services let you lock in a rate at the moment you send; others apply the rate when the money arrives. If the rupee is moving fast, a locked rate removes the guesswork.
  • Send on weekdays when markets are open. Transfers sent over the weekend may be processed at Monday's rate, which could be better or worse. If the amount is large, a weekday transfer gives you more control.
  • Keep the receipt and the tracking number. Your family should be able to check the transfer status, and you need the record if anything goes wrong. The free guides on CyberSec Hub explain how to check that a money transfer website or app is genuine before you trust it with your money.
  • Think about timing, but do not gamble. If the rupee has weakened sharply, waiting a few days sometimes gets a better rate — but it can also get worse. Never delay money your family needs for a rate guess, and never borrow to send money in the hope the rate will improve.

Travel money: buying foreign currency for a trip

Travellers lose money on currency more often than on anything else on a trip — usually in small amounts that add up. A few habits keep the cost down:

  • Buy before you travel. Exchange your rupees at a licensed money changer in Pakistan before you fly. Airport exchange counters and hotel desks almost always give the worst rates in the world.
  • Buy only what you need in cash. Take enough foreign cash for taxis, tips and small purchases, and rely on your card for the rest. Carrying large amounts of cash is risky, and changing leftover foreign currency back into rupees costs you the spread twice.
  • Tell your bank you are travelling. A card blocked for "suspicious activity" in a foreign country is a miserable experience. Inform your bank of your travel dates in advance, and keep the bank's helpline number saved separately from your wallet.
  • Pay in the local currency, not in rupees. When a card machine abroad offers to charge you in rupees, always choose the local currency. The machine's conversion rate is almost always worse than your bank's.
  • Check the weather and flight before you go. A trip is more than money: check the weather at your destination and your live flight status on GlobalStream before you leave, and if you are travelling for Umrah or Hajj, the prayer times for your destination city are on the same site.
  • Keep some emergency money separate. A small amount of cash in a different pocket or bag, plus a second card, can save a trip if your wallet is lost or stolen.

Exchange scams to avoid

  • "Better than market" street dealers. Anyone offering a rate far better than every licensed money changer is either dealing in counterfeit notes or planning to rob you. Exchange only at licensed shops with a visible licence and a proper receipt.
  • Fake rate screenshots. Scammers circulate edited screenshots showing a wonderful rate, then ask for advance payment online. Real rates are live and checkable — if you cannot verify the rate on a live page like the GlobalStream currency page, do not send money.
  • Counterfeit notes. Count the money in front of the dealer, check the security features of the notes, and do not accept notes that look washed out, torn or strangely smooth. If a dealer's notes look wrong, walk away.
  • The "short count" trick. Some dishonest dealers count the money quickly and hand over less than agreed, hoping you will not recount. Always recount slowly, in front of them, before you leave the shop.
  • Fake exchange apps and websites. Criminals clone real money transfer sites to steal logins and payments. Before using any new exchange app, check it against the safety guides on CyberSec Hub, and never enter your bank details from a link in a message — type the address yourself.
  • Informal transfer networks. Unlicensed transfer systems may seem cheaper, but your money has no protection, no record and no legal remedy if it disappears. The small saving is not worth the risk.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the interbank rate and the open market rate?

The interbank rate is the wholesale rate banks use with each other — it is the benchmark you see in the news. The open market rate is what licensed money changers charge ordinary customers in cash. The open market rate is usually slightly worse, because the dealer has costs and a profit margin. Always know which rate you are looking at before comparing prices.

Why does the dollar rate change every day?

Because supply and demand for dollars change every day — with trade, remittances, interest rates, global dollar moves and market confidence. Small daily moves are normal; large sudden moves usually mean important news, which you can follow on the Pakistan news page and the world news page.

Where is the best place to exchange currency in Pakistan?

For cash, a licensed money changer in a main commercial area — compare two or three before a large amount. For transfers, compare banks and licensed transfer companies on the total cost: fee plus exchange rate. Avoid airports, hotels and street dealers, which consistently give the worst rates.

Is it better to send dollars or rupees to Pakistan?

Most overseas Pakistanis send foreign currency and let it convert to rupees on arrival, because the conversion rate on arrival is usually better than converting abroad. But the best answer depends on the service you use — compare the total rupees your family receives, not the advertised fee.

Should I buy dollars as an investment?

Holding dollars can protect savings when the rupee weakens, but currencies move both ways, and keeping large amounts of foreign cash at home is risky and unproductive. Many Pakistanis split their savings between rupees, gold and — for younger, risk-tolerant savers — a small amount of cryptocurrency, which is far more volatile than either. Never borrow money to buy currency, and never put all your savings into one thing. For the bigger picture on gold as an alternative, see our guide to the gold rate in Pakistan.

The bottom line

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in another, and in Pakistan you will always meet two versions of it: the interbank benchmark and the open market rate you actually pay. The smart way to deal with it is to check the live rate at the moment you need it, understand buying versus selling and the spread, compare two or three licensed dealers or services, and never trust a rate you cannot verify on a live page. Check the live currency rates, keep an eye on the gold rate and world news for what is moving markets, and use official channels for every transfer — your money, and your family's money, deserves nothing less.
