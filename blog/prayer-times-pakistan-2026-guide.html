Prayer Times in Pakistan: How to Check Accurate Timings (2026 Guide)

By Anwaar Digital · Last updated: 13 November 2026 · Daily life & prayer

For Muslims in Pakistan, the five daily prayers are the rhythm of the day. Fajr begins the morning, Dhuhr breaks the middle of the workday, Asr comes in the afternoon, Maghrib closes the day at sunset, and Isha ends it at night. Yet many people are unsure about the exact times — one app says one thing, the mosque timetable says another, and a printed calendar from last year says something else again. Which one is right? And why do the times change a little every single day?

This guide explains prayer times in Pakistan in plain English: what the five prayers are, how their times are calculated, why different sources disagree, and how to check accurate timings for your own city — for free, in seconds. This article deliberately prints no clock times of its own, because prayer times change daily and differ from city to city. Any specific time printed here would be wrong for most readers within days. Instead, it shows you where to check the live times whenever you need them — starting with the live prayer times page on GlobalStream.

What are the five daily prayers?

Muslims pray five times a day, and each prayer has a window of time in which it should be performed. The times are fixed by the position of the sun, not by the clock — which is why they move a few minutes earlier or later every day, and why they are different in Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad and Peshawar.

  • Fajr (dawn prayer). Begins at true dawn, when the first light spreads across the horizon, and ends just before sunrise. It is the earliest prayer of the day and the one whose start time causes the most confusion, because it depends on a precise angle of the sun below the horizon (explained below).
  • Dhuhr (midday prayer). Begins just after the sun has passed its highest point in the sky (the zenith) and starts to decline. Its window runs until Asr begins. In Pakistan, Dhuhr is usually prayed in the early afternoon, and mosques often announce it with the azan a few minutes after the calculated time.
  • Asr (afternoon prayer). Begins when the shadow of an object reaches a certain length — in the Hanafi school followed by most Muslims in Pakistan, when the shadow is twice the object's length plus the shadow at noon. It ends just before sunset. Asr is the prayer most affected by which school of thought a timetable follows, so it is the one that most often differs between two sources.
  • Maghrib (sunset prayer). Begins the moment the sun has fully set below the horizon. Its window is short — it ends when the twilight disappears and Isha begins. Maghrib is also the time for breaking the fast in Ramadan, which is why its exact minute matters so much during the holy month.
  • Isha (night prayer). Begins when the twilight has fully disappeared and the night sky is dark. Its window runs until the middle of the night by most calculations, though it is best prayed earlier in its window. Like Fajr, its start depends on a sun angle below the horizon, so different methods give different Isha times.

On Fridays, Dhuhr is replaced by the Jumu'ah (Friday) prayer, prayed in congregation at the mosque. The Jumu'ah time is usually the same as Dhuhr, but mosques may set their own congregation time — check your local mosque's notice board rather than relying on an app for Jumu'ah.

How are prayer times calculated?

Prayer times are not decided by a committee or guessed by an app. They are calculated from astronomy: the position of the sun for your exact latitude and longitude, on your exact date. A calculation needs four things — where you are, what day it is, which method is being used, and how much rounding is applied.

The sun decides the time

Each prayer is tied to a solar event. Dhuhr follows the sun passing its highest point. Asr follows the length of shadows. Maghrib follows sunset. Fajr and Isha follow the sun sitting a certain number of degrees below the horizon before sunrise and after sunset. Because the sun's path changes through the year, the times shift a little every day — longer days in summer, shorter days in winter. That daily shift is normal, not a mistake.

Calculation methods — and why they disagree

The one place where honest sources disagree is Fajr and Isha, because "how far below the horizon" is a matter of definition. Different respected organisations use different angles:

  • Muslim World League: Fajr at 18 degrees below the horizon, Isha at 17 degrees.
  • Islamic Society of North America (ISNA): 15 degrees for both Fajr and Isha.
  • Umm al-Qura University (Makkah): Fajr at 18.5 degrees; Isha fixed at 90 minutes after Maghrib.
  • University of Islamic Sciences, Karachi: 18 degrees for both Fajr and Isha — the method most commonly used in Pakistan.

A one-degree difference sounds tiny, but it can move Fajr by several minutes. That is the main reason two apps on the same phone can show different Fajr and Isha times for the same city on the same day. Neither is "wrong" — they are using different methods. In Pakistan, most mosques and timetables follow the Karachi method (18 degrees), so a timetable that matches your local mosque is usually using that method. When you use an app or a website, check which method it uses — the GlobalStream prayer times page shows the method and the calculation details so you know exactly what you are looking at.

Asr: Hanafi vs Shafi'i

The second place sources disagree is Asr. The Hanafi school, followed by the large majority of Muslims in Pakistan, starts Asr when an object's shadow is twice its length (plus the noon shadow). The Shafi'i, Maliki and Hanbali schools start Asr earlier, when the shadow equals the object's length. The difference is usually 30 to 45 minutes. If an app shows an Asr time much earlier than your mosque's timetable, it is probably set to the Shafi'i method. Most apps and websites let you choose — set yours to Hanafi if that is what your mosque follows.

Why do prayer times differ between cities and apps?

There are four honest reasons, and one dishonest one.

1. Different cities, different sun

Prayer times depend on your exact location. Lahore is further east than Karachi, so its sun rises and sets earlier — Lahore's Fajr is earlier than Karachi's. Islamabad is further north, so its day length differs again, especially in winter. A difference of a few minutes between cities is expected and correct. Never use another city's timetable: even cities a short drive apart can differ by a minute or two, and that minute matters for Fajr and Maghrib.

2. Different methods

As explained above, the Fajr and Isha angles and the Asr rule vary by method. Two apps can both be correct and still disagree by several minutes. Check the method setting before you decide one of them is wrong.

3. Rounding and safety margins

Many printed timetables and mosque boards add a small safety margin — a minute or two — to Fajr and Maghrib, so that nobody prays a minute too early. Apps usually show the raw calculated time. A one- or two-minute difference between a mosque board and an app is often just this margin, and the mosque board is the safer one to follow for congregation.

4. Outdated timetables

A printed calendar from last year, a timetable photo saved on your phone, or a website that has not updated its data will be wrong — prayer times move every day. Always check a live source for today's times, not a saved or printed one from another date.

5. The dishonest reason: fake or careless apps

Some apps show copied, guessed or badly calculated times, and a few exist mainly to show ads. If an app's times disagree with your mosque by a large amount — ten minutes or more — and it does not say which method it uses, stop trusting it. Use a source that names its method and shows its calculation, like the GlobalStream prayer times page, and cross-check against your mosque for a week.

Fajr and Isha angles, explained simply

The angle numbers in the methods above confuse many people, so here is the simple version. Imagine the sun sinking below the horizon at sunset. It does not get dark instantly — the sky stays lit for a while. That fading light is called twilight. Isha begins when the twilight has completely gone and the sky is fully dark. Fajr begins when the first light of dawn appears in the east, before the sun itself rises.

The "angle" is simply how far below the horizon the sun must be for that moment to count. A bigger angle means the sun must sink further down — so Fajr starts later in the morning (the sun has to climb further to reach that angle) and Isha starts later at night. A smaller angle means Fajr starts earlier and Isha starts earlier. The commonly used 18-degree method waits for a darker sky than the 15-degree method, so it gives a slightly later Fajr and a slightly later Isha.

Why does it matter? Because Fajr and Isha are the two prayers with no obvious sky event you can see clearly — unlike Maghrib, where you can watch the sun set. That is why scholars defined them by angle, and why different scholars chose slightly different angles. There is no single "correct" angle that all scholars agree on, which is why methods differ — and why following your local mosque's method is the practical answer for most people.

Ramadan and seasonal shifts

Prayer times move with the seasons, and two times of year deserve special attention.

Through the year

In summer, days are long: Fajr is very early, Isha is late, and the gap between Maghrib and Isha is wide. In winter, days are short: Fajr is later, Isha is earlier, and Maghrib and Isha are close together. In Pakistan the swing is large — Fajr can move by more than an hour between June and December. That is why a fixed clock time for Fajr can never work: a time that is right in January is wrong by June. Check the live times daily, especially in the weeks when the seasons change and the times move fastest.

Ramadan

In Ramadan, the two times that matter most are Fajr and Maghrib: Suhoor (the pre-dawn meal) must end before Fajr begins, and the fast is broken at Maghrib. Mosques and media announce special Ramadan timetables, and most people follow their mosque's Suhoor and Iftar times rather than an app. Two cautions: first, make sure your app or page is using the same method as the Ramadan timetable you are following, or your Fajr may disagree by a few minutes; second, if you are travelling during Ramadan, remember that Iftar time changes with the city — breaking your fast by Karachi's time while you are in Lahore, or the other way round, will be wrong by several minutes. Check the live prayer times for the city you are in, and if you are flying home for Iftar, check the live flight status first so a delay does not catch you in the air at Maghrib.

Setting reminders so you never miss a prayer

Knowing the times is only half the job — remembering them in a busy day is the other half. A few habits help:

  • Set an alarm for Fajr the night before. Fajr moves a little every day, so a fixed alarm time slowly drifts out of date. Check the live time the evening before and set the alarm to it, or use an app that updates the alarm automatically from the daily calculation.
  • Use the azan as your reminder. If you live near a mosque, the azan itself is the reminder — keep a window open or step outside at the expected time rather than relying on memory. In areas without a nearby mosque, a prayer app with azan notifications does the same job.
  • Build prayers into your workday. Dhuhr and Asr fall inside working hours for most people. Agree with your employer or colleagues on a short break, keep a prayer mat at work, and use the time between Dhuhr and Asr for the tasks that need the most focus. Many offices in Pakistan already allow this — ask if you have not.
  • Use the tools already on your phone. A calendar reminder set to repeat daily will go stale as the times move, so prefer an app or a live page that follows the daily calculation. The free tools on Anwaar Free Tools include calculators that can help you plan your day around the prayer windows.
  • When in doubt, pray early in the window. Every prayer has a window, not a single instant. Praying at the start of the window is the safest choice — it protects you if the time you were following was a minute or two late.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using another city's times. The most common mistake. A Karachi timetable used in Lahore, or a Lahore app used in Islamabad, will be wrong by several minutes. Always set your actual city.
  • Trusting an old printed timetable. Prayer times shift daily. A timetable from last month — let alone last year — is wrong. Check a live source.
  • Following the wrong method. An app set to the Shafi'i Asr rule or a 15-degree Fajr will disagree with your mosque. Match the method to your mosque.
  • Confusing azan time with prayer time. The azan is usually called a few minutes after the prayer time begins. The prayer time itself is the calculated moment; the azan is the announcement. For fasting, the calculated Fajr time is what counts, not the azan.
  • Forgetting the date when travelling. When you fly across time zones — for Umrah, Hajj, work or study — your phone may still show your home city's times, or the wrong date entirely. Frequently asked questions

Why does my app show a different Fajr time from my mosque?

Almost always because of the calculation method. Mosques in Pakistan generally use the University of Islamic Sciences, Karachi method (18 degrees for Fajr and Isha) with the Hanafi Asr rule. Check your app's method setting and match it to your mosque — the difference will usually disappear.

Why do prayer times change every day?

Because they follow the sun, and the sun's path changes daily through the year. Days get longer towards June and shorter towards December, so every prayer time moves a little each day. A fixed clock time would be wrong within days.

Why is Asr different on my app from my mosque?

Because of the Hanafi vs Shafi'i rule. The Hanafi method, followed by most mosques in Pakistan, starts Asr later (shadow twice the object's length). An app set to the Shafi'i rule starts Asr earlier. Change the app's Asr setting to Hanafi.

What is the safest source for prayer times in Pakistan?

Your local mosque's timetable, cross-checked against a live calculation page that names its method — such as the GlobalStream prayer times page. If the two agree within a minute or two for a week, you have a source you can trust.

Do prayer times change with daylight saving?

Pakistan does not currently observe daylight saving time, so the clock does not shift. But if you travel to a country that does — such as the UK, Europe or parts of the US — the local clock moves by an hour and the prayer times shift with it. Set your app to the city you are in and let it handle the change.

Prayer times in Pakistan follow the sun: they change a little every day, differ from city to city, and depend on the calculation method for Fajr, Isha and Asr. The smart way to follow them is to use a live source that names its method, match the method to your local mosque, set your actual city — and check again when you travel.
