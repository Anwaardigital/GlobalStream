Cryptocurrency Prices in Pakistan: How to Read Them and Stay Safe (2026 Guide)

By Anwaar Digital · Last updated: 12 November 2026 · Money & crypto

Bitcoin, Ethereum, USDT — you have heard the names in the news, in WhatsApp groups, and from friends who say they made money, or lost it. In Pakistan, interest in cryptocurrency keeps growing: young people follow the prices daily, overseas Pakistanis ask about sending crypto home, and traders watch the charts late into the night. Yet crypto is also one of the most misunderstood topics in the country. What actually is a cryptocurrency? Why does the price of Bitcoin swing so violently? Is crypto legal in Pakistan? And how do you tell a genuine opportunity from a scam?

This guide explains cryptocurrency prices in Pakistan in plain English: what the main coins are, how prices are set, how to read a price chart, what moves the market, the legal picture in Pakistan, and how to buy safely if you decide to. What is a cryptocurrency?

A cryptocurrency is a form of digital money that is not issued or controlled by any government or central bank. Instead of a bank keeping the records, transactions are recorded on a public digital ledger called a blockchain, which is maintained by thousands of computers around the world. Nobody can spend the same coin twice, and nobody can change the records after the fact — that is the whole point of the system.

There are thousands of cryptocurrencies, but only a few matter to most people in Pakistan:

  • Bitcoin (BTC). The first and largest cryptocurrency, created in 2009. It is often called "digital gold" because its supply is limited — only 21 million bitcoins will ever exist. When people say "the crypto market is up" or "down", they usually mean Bitcoin first of all.
  • Ethereum (ETH). The second-largest cryptocurrency. As well as being a currency, Ethereum is a platform that other crypto projects are built on — most tokens, apps and digital collectibles run on it or on networks like it.
  • Stablecoins (USDT, USDC). Cryptocurrencies designed to hold a steady value, usually one US dollar per coin. USDT (Tether) is the one most used in Pakistan. People use stablecoins to hold dollars digitally, to trade, and to send value across borders without a bank. A stablecoin is only as good as the company behind it and the reserves it claims to hold, so not all stablecoins are equally safe.
  • Altcoins. Everything else — thousands of smaller coins with different purposes, from fast-payment coins to tokens for games and apps. Most of them are far riskier and far more volatile than Bitcoin or Ethereum, and many eventually lose almost all their value.

A useful way to think about it: Bitcoin is like digital gold, Ethereum is like a digital computer network that other things are built on, and stablecoins are like digital dollars. Everything else is a bet on a much smaller, much riskier project.

How are crypto prices set?

Unlike the rupee, whose value is managed by the State Bank, crypto prices are set purely by supply and demand on exchanges — the websites and apps where people buy and sell. When more people want to buy a coin than sell it, the price rises. When more people want to sell, the price falls. There is no central bank, no official rate, and no one who steps in to steady the price.

Three features make crypto prices different from anything else you may follow on GlobalStream:

  • The market never closes. Stock markets close at night and on weekends. Crypto trades 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, all year. A price can crash at 3 a.m. while you are asleep. That is why a "live" price page matters so much more for crypto than for anything else.
  • Volatility is extreme. A 5% move in a day is a big day for the dollar-rupee rate; for Bitcoin it is an ordinary day, and smaller coins can move 20% or 30% in a few hours. The same force that can double your money in a month can cut it in half just as fast. Anyone who promises you steady, guaranteed returns from crypto is lying — the market does not work that way.
  • Prices differ slightly between exchanges. Because there is no single official price, Bitcoin may cost a little more on one exchange than on another at the same moment. The difference is usually small for big coins, but it is real — and it is one reason to check a live aggregator like the GlobalStream crypto page, which shows the market-wide picture rather than one exchange's price.

Crypto prices are usually quoted in US dollars, and in Pakistan people then convert to rupees in their heads. Remember that this means the rupee price of Bitcoin moves for two reasons at once: the dollar price of Bitcoin, and the rupee-dollar rate. If Bitcoin is flat in dollars but the rupee weakens, Bitcoin becomes more expensive in rupees. Keep an eye on both — the live dollar rate and the live crypto prices — to understand what you are really paying.

How to read a crypto price table

A crypto price page looks busy the first time, but it has only a few numbers that matter. Once you know them, you can read any crypto table in seconds.

  • Price. The current price of one coin, usually in US dollars. On GlobalStream you can see the price in dollars and work out the rupee value using the live dollar rate.
  • 24-hour change. How much the price has moved in the last 24 hours, shown as a percentage and usually coloured green (up) or red (down). A coin up 8% in a day is hot; a coin down 8% in a day is falling fast. But one day tells you very little — always look at the longer picture too.
  • Market cap (market capitalisation). The total value of all coins in existence — price multiplied by the number of coins. Bitcoin's market cap is far larger than any other coin's, which is why it moves more slowly and is considered less risky. A coin with a tiny market cap can double on a small amount of buying — and can collapse just as easily.
  • 24-hour trading volume. How much of the coin was bought and sold in the last day. High volume means lots of people are trading it, which usually means you can buy and sell easily. Low volume is a warning sign: a coin nobody trades can be hard to sell when you need to, and its price can be pushed around by a few big traders.
  • High and low. The highest and lowest price in the last 24 hours (or 7 days). This tells you how wild the ride has been. A coin that traded between two prices far apart in one day is a coin that can hurt you quickly.
  • The chart. The price line over time — 1 day, 7 days, 1 month, 1 year. Look at the 1-year chart before you buy anything: a coin that is down 80% from its peak is not "cheap", it may be dying. And a coin that has gone straight up for a month is not "safe", it may be about to fall. Charts show you history, not the future — but buying without looking at the chart is like driving with your eyes closed.

One practical rule: never buy a coin because of a single green number. Check the price, the market cap, the volume and the 1-year chart together. If any of them looks wrong — tiny market cap, almost no trading, a chart that only goes down — walk away, no matter what anyone in a group chat tells you.

What moves crypto prices?

Crypto prices do not move randomly. Several forces push them up or down, and most of them are visible in the news if you know what to look for.

1. Bitcoin leads, the rest follow

Bitcoin is roughly half the entire crypto market by value, and almost every other coin follows its direction. When Bitcoin rises, most coins rise; when Bitcoin falls, most coins fall harder. Before you study a small coin, check what Bitcoin is doing — it sets the weather for the whole market.

2. Interest rates and the global dollar

When interest rates in the US are high, investors can earn safe returns from bank deposits and government bonds, so risky assets like crypto become less attractive. When rates fall, money flows back into riskier things, including crypto. The same global dollar moves that push the rupee around — which you can follow on the world news page — also push crypto prices. Crypto and the dollar often move in opposite directions.

3. News, regulation and big announcements

A government banning crypto, a big company accepting Bitcoin, a major exchange being hacked, or a new crypto fund launching — each of these can move prices by double digits in a day. Crypto reacts to news faster and harder than any other market, because the market never sleeps and traders react within minutes. Follow world news and Pakistan news if you hold crypto: the headline that moves your coins is often not a crypto headline at all.

4. Supply events

Bitcoin's new supply is cut in half roughly every four years in an event called the "halving". History shows that halvings are followed by strong price moves over the following year, though nothing is guaranteed and each cycle is different. Other coins have their own supply changes — new coins released, coins locked up, or coins "burned" (destroyed). Before buying a coin, find out how its supply works: a coin whose supply grows fast will struggle to rise in price, because new coins keep diluting the old ones.

5. Fear and greed

Crypto is the most emotional market in the world. When prices rise, new buyers rush in because they are afraid of missing out — and push prices higher. When prices fall, the same people panic and sell — and push prices lower. The pattern repeats in every cycle. The investors who survive are the ones who decide their plan in advance — how much to buy, when to sell, how much they can afford to lose — and do not change it because of a green or red day.

6. Gold, the dollar and crypto move together — sometimes

In uncertain times, people buy gold, dollars and sometimes Bitcoin as a shelter. At other times, crypto moves with risky assets like tech shares, not with gold at all. The relationship changes, which is why it is worth watching all three together: the live gold rate, the live dollar rate and the live crypto prices on GlobalStream. If gold and the dollar are both rising sharply, the world is nervous — and crypto usually feels it too.

Crypto in Pakistan: the legal and practical picture

This is the question every Pakistani asks first, and it deserves a straight answer: cryptocurrency is not legal tender in Pakistan, and the State Bank of Pakistan has told banks and payment companies not to deal in virtual currencies. That means you cannot walk into a bank and buy Bitcoin, and no Pakistani bank will help you trade crypto. However, there is no law that clearly makes simply owning crypto a crime for an ordinary person, and buying and selling happens widely through peer-to-peer (P2P) trading — one person selling crypto to another, with payment by bank transfer or cash.

The situation is unsettled and can change. Governments in Pakistan have discussed regulating crypto, taxing it, and even launching official frameworks, and the rules in other countries change every year too. Because the picture can change quickly, treat this section as a snapshot of today, not a permanent rule:

  • Do not assume crypto is "legal" or "illegal" in simple terms. It sits in a grey area: not legal tender, not supported by banks, but widely traded person-to-person. Anyone who tells you it is fully legal, or fully banned, is oversimplifying.
  • Keep up with the news. Any change in Pakistan's crypto rules will be major news. Follow the Pakistan news page on GlobalStream and official State Bank announcements, not rumours in trading groups.
  • Your bank can freeze a transfer it does not understand. Banks in Pakistan monitor transfers, and a payment marked or described as crypto-related can be delayed, questioned or blocked. Never mislabel a payment or use someone else's account to hide what a transfer is for — that creates bigger problems than the trade itself.
  • You are on your own if something goes wrong. Because banks do not support crypto, there is no bank to complain to if a trade goes wrong or an exchange disappears with your money. That is the single biggest practical difference from normal banking, and it is why the safety rules in the next section matter so much.
  • Never use crypto to hide money or dodge the law. Crypto's privacy features attract criminals, and tax authorities and banks around the world are increasingly able to trace crypto transactions. Use crypto openly, keep records, and pay whatever tax applies — guidance on staying safe online, including how to spot fake crypto platforms, is on CyberSec Hub.

How to buy crypto safely in Pakistan

Most Pakistanis who buy crypto do it through P2P trading on a large international exchange: you find a seller on the platform, pay them by bank transfer, and the platform releases the crypto from escrow once the payment is confirmed. Done carefully, it works. Done carelessly, it is how people lose money. Follow these rules:

  • Use a large, well-known exchange — never a random website or a person from a WhatsApp group. Big exchanges hold the seller's crypto in escrow, so the seller cannot take your money and vanish. A deal outside a platform has no protection at all.
  • Check the trader's history. On P2P platforms, every trader has a rating, a number of completed trades, and a completion rate. Only trade with sellers who have hundreds of completed trades and a completion rate above 95%. A new account with no history offering a wonderful rate is a trap, not a bargain.
  • Never release payment outside the platform. The escrow system only protects you if the money and the chat stay inside the exchange. If a seller asks you to pay by a different method, or to chat on WhatsApp or Telegram instead, stop the trade immediately.
  • Start small. Your first trade should be a small amount — enough to learn how the process works, small enough that losing it would not hurt. Only increase the amount once you have completed several trades without problems.
  • Protect your account like your bank account — because it is one. Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA) with an authenticator app, not SMS. Use a unique, strong password you use nowhere else. Never share your login, your 2FA codes, or your wallet's recovery phrase with anyone — not even someone claiming to be "support". Real support will never ask for them.
  • Move large holdings to your own wallet. Crypto left on an exchange belongs to the exchange's systems, not to you — and exchanges have been hacked and have collapsed. For amounts you plan to hold for months or years, learn how a personal wallet works (a hardware wallet is the safest option) and keep your recovery phrase written on paper, stored somewhere safe, never photographed or stored online.
  • Only invest money you can afford to lose completely. This is not a slogan — it is the only sane rule for crypto. Never borrow money to buy crypto, never sell essential things to buy it, and never put in money your family needs for rent, fees or food. Crypto can fall 50% in a few weeks, and it has done so many times.
  • Write everything down. Keep a record of every trade: date, coin, amount, price, platform, and the other party. If tax rules change or a dispute arises, records are your only protection. The free tools on Anwaar Free Tools can help you calculate and track amounts before you commit to a trade.

Scams to avoid

Pakistanis lose more money to crypto scams than to bad trades. The scams follow the same patterns again and again — learn them once and you will recognise them forever:

  • "Double your money" schemes. Anyone promising to double your Bitcoin in a week, a month, or any fixed time is running a fraud. Real markets do not work that way. These schemes pay early "investors" with later investors' money until they collapse — and the last people in lose everything.
  • Fake investment groups. Telegram and WhatsApp groups showing screenshots of huge profits, "signals" that always win, and members thanking the admin are usually staged. The screenshots are fake, the members are fake accounts, and the "admin" disappears once enough people have paid.
  • Giveaway scams. "Send 0.1 BTC and receive 0.2 BTC back" — seen in fake celebrity accounts, hacked YouTube channels and comment sections. Nobody gives away free crypto. Every coin you send is gone.
  • Fake exchanges and fake apps. Criminals clone real exchange websites and apps to steal logins and deposits. Only download an exchange app from the official app store, check the developer name, and type the website address yourself — never click a link in a message, an ad or a search result without checking it carefully. The safety guides on CyberSec Hub explain how to check whether a website or app is genuine.
  • Fake support. Someone messages you claiming to be exchange support, asking you to "verify" your wallet by sharing your recovery phrase or by sending crypto to a "safe" address. Real support never does this. Your recovery phrase is the key to your money: anyone who has it owns your coins.
  • Pump-and-dump coins. A group hypes a tiny, unknown coin, the price shoots up as members buy, then the organisers sell everything and the price collapses. If you hear about a coin only from people who are trying to sell it to you, you are the exit plan, not the opportunity.
  • Fake "airdrops" and free tokens. Websites offering free coins in exchange for connecting your wallet can drain it in seconds. Never connect your wallet to a site you do not fully trust, and never approve a transaction you do not understand.

If an offer makes you feel rushed, excited or afraid of missing out, that feeling is the scam working. Slow down, check the price on the live crypto page, search for the scheme's name plus the word "scam", and ask someone you trust. The money you do not lose to a scam is worth exactly as much as the money you earn from a good trade.

Frequently asked questions

Is Bitcoin legal in Pakistan?

Bitcoin is not legal tender in Pakistan, and banks are not allowed to deal in it — but there is no clear law making it a crime for an ordinary person to own it, and P2P trading is widespread. The rules are unsettled and may change, so follow Pakistan news and official State Bank statements rather than rumours.

Where can I check the live Bitcoin price in rupees?

Right here on GlobalStream: the crypto page shows live prices for Bitcoin, Ethereum, USDT and other major coins, updated constantly. Use the live dollar rate to work out the rupee value at the moment you check.

What is USDT and why do Pakistanis use it?

USDT (Tether) is a stablecoin designed to stay close to one US dollar. Many Pakistanis use it to hold dollars digitally, to trade between coins without touching a bank, and to receive payments from abroad. It is convenient — but it is not a bank deposit, and its safety depends on the company behind it.

How much money do I need to start?

Very little: most exchanges let you buy a fraction of a coin, so you can start with a small amount and learn. The right question is not how little you can start with, but how much you can afford to lose — never invest money your family needs.

Why did Bitcoin fall so suddenly?

Usually one of the forces above: a negative news event, rising interest rates, a big holder selling, or a wave of panic after a fall. Crypto falls fast because the market never closes and leveraged traders are forced to sell. Check the world news page for what happened, and remember that a fall is not proof a coin is finished — nor is a rise proof it will keep rising.

Is crypto a good investment?

It can be, for a small part of a balanced plan, held for years, by someone who understands the risk. It is a bad idea as a get-rich-quick scheme, as a loan-funded gamble, or as the place for money you cannot afford to lose. Many Pakistanis balance crypto with steadier stores of value — see our guides to the gold rate in Pakistan and currency exchange rates for the full picture.

Can I send crypto to family abroad?

Technically yes — that is one of crypto's main uses — but the legal position, the fees, and the risk of the price moving while the transfer is in progress all need care. For most family support, a normal bank or licensed remittance transfer is simpler, safer and easier to prove. If you do use crypto, use a stablecoin, not a volatile coin, and only through a reputable platform.

The bottom line

Cryptocurrency is real technology with real uses — and real risks. Prices are set by supply and demand on markets that never close, they can move further in a day than the rupee moves in a year, and the legal picture in Pakistan is still unsettled. The smart way to approach it is to learn how to read a price table properly, understand what moves the market, start small, use only large reputable platforms, protect your account and your recovery phrase, and never invest money you cannot afford to lose. Check the live crypto prices whenever you need a number, keep an eye on the gold rate and the dollar rate for the bigger picture, and treat every "guaranteed profit" promise as the scam it is.
